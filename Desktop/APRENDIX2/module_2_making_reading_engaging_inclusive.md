### 2. Learning Objectives (2–3)

* Teachers use interactive routines to make reading meaningful with limited texts or materials.
* Teachers implement peer discussion to promote reading as a shared experience.
* Teachers encourage comprehension, confidence, and participation through shared readings and finger tracking.

### 3. Module Rules

* Prioritize how one text can serve many learners
* Encourage teacher modeling reading strategies

### 4. Key Concepts

* **READING_M2_CON1 / Engagement supports understanding.** Children understand stories better when they are actively involved in reading. Asking questions, making predictions, and responding during reading helps learners stay focused and connected to the text. Engagement helps prevent students from becoming distracted, especially in large classrooms. When students are involved, reading becomes meaningful instead of passive. Active participation allows teachers to quickly see who is understanding and who may need additional support. Engagement helps learners feel confident, and embrace the process of learning how to read.

* **READING_M2_CON2 / One text can reach many learners.** One short text can support learners at different reading levels. When teachers reread, point to the words while reading, and offer different methods of participation, all learners are included. Some learners may listen, read together, or read independently. A single text can be a flexible tool, especially in learning environments where books may be limited. Teachers can use differentiation to ensure students can not only engage with a shared text, but also learn from fellow students.

* **READING_M2_CON3 / Supported practice develops fluency**. When students have repeated, supported opportunities to read, fluency develops. Hearing a teacher read a text gives students a clear model of how reading should sound. Echo and choral reading allow students to practice reading without fear of being singled out. This support is especially important for learners who feel nervous or anxious about reading. Practice helps students hone their reading skills and develop greater confidence in their abilities. When practice feels safe and supportive, students are more willing to keep trying.

* **READING_M2_CON4 / Sharing builds comprehension**. Students understand stories better when they share their thoughts and ideas with others. Sharing with a partner or group helps learners organize their thinking and develop their oral language skills. Verbally communicating ideas is a helpful alternative for students who struggle to express their ideas in writing. Talking about a text develops students' analytical skills as they clarify meaning, remember events, and make connections. When students share ideas, reading becomes an experience that builds understanding and belonging.

### 5. Strategies

#### READING_M2_STRAT1 / Strategy 1: Picture Walks

* **Description:** Looking through pictures before reading to build background knowledge, review vocabulary, and make predictions.
* **Expanded Explanation:** Picture walks prepare students to understand a story before they hear the words. Teachers display each page of a text to their students, prompting students to name objects, actions, and feelings in the pictures. This introduces students to the setting, characters, and sequence of the text. Picture walks are done at a slower pace to allow students the opportunity to really look at the pictures. Teachers should ask open-ended questions to engage students in critical thinking (e.g. "What do you notice about how the character is feeling on this page? I wonder what changed." "What do you think may happen next?" "Where do you think the characters are?"). Pausing throughout the picture walk and asking questions also allows students to make predictions about the outcomes of the story. Teachers should review the text before a lesson and make note of good places to pause, prep questions to ask, and write key vocabulary words to review with students. If a book uses terms that students do not already know, a picture walk is a time to introduce the word (e.g. "In this book, you will hear me say the word 'giggle.' Does anyone know what this word means?. . . It is another way to say laugh. Can we practice giggling right now?. . .Nice! While I read, I want you to listen for the word giggle").
* **Examples / Variations:**
  * When no book is available, teachers can draw simple pictures on the board to depict the beginning, middle, and end of the oral story.
  * Create vocabulary cards for the new words that will be featured in the book to post on the wall and/or have students hold up the cards when they hear the word being read.
  * Write student predictions on the board and reflect on them after the reading
  * Teacher does not show the final pages of the book and asks students to draw what they think will happen
* **Reflection Prompt:** How do making predictions affect attention during reading?
* **Teacher Voice:** "My students are more focused when we review pictures before reading."

#### READING_M2_STRAT2 / Strategy 2: Shared Reading and Finger Tracking

* **Description:** Reading a shared text while tracking each word as it is said
* **Expanded Explanation:** Writing a short text on the board or on a poster invites all learners to practice reading. The text can be simple and feature vocabulary and high-frequency words that have recently been taught in reading class. Pointing to the words as they're read (also known as finger tracking) helps students see that spoken words match written words, along with noticing direction, spacing, and handwriting. When guiding the class in reading the shared text, teachers model finger tracking and instruct students to track in the air with their finger. This keeps all learners included, even without their own text. This is a strategy that works best when built into the weekly routine of a reading class (e.g. Every Monday, a new shared text is posted for the class to use and revisit throughout the week).
* **Examples / Variations:**
  * Teacher uses a long stick to track the written words on the board so that students can view the full text clearly
  * Students track the text with their finger in the air or by tapping on their desk
  * At the end of the week, teacher invites students who are able to read the text independently to lead the tracking at the board
  * Teacher writes the text on a slate or paper, works with students individually or in small groups to practice tracking on printed text
* **Reflection Prompt:** How might tracking change the pace of reading?
* **Teacher Voice:** "Even without their own book, my students could follow the words."

#### READING_M2_STRAT3 / Strategy 3: Echo→Choral→Independent Reading

* **Description:** Guiding students to read by repeating, read together, and read independently
* **Expanded Explanation:** This reading strategy builds student confidence step by step. Teachers can use a short story or paragraph (2 to 5 sentences) as the text for echo-choral-independent reading. The teacher should first introduce the text to their students (e.g "Today we will be reading a text called 'Going to the farm.' Before we read, what do you think will happen in the story?"). The teacher will then read a line of the story and students echo, or repeat, the line. This allows students to hear fluent reading first. Teachers should read slowly and clearly to help students understand the text. Next is choral reading, the teacher and students will read the same line again but together. Reading the line again with the teachers allows all students to practice without pressure. Then, the teacher allows students to try independent reading, where they can read the line by themselves. Not all students will feel comfortable reading independently. The teacher should encourage students to join independent reading based on their own comfort level. Reading should not be a place of shame or embarrassment. When teachers emphasize effort over perfection, students are more willing to take risks, practice fluency, and grow as readers.
* **Examples / Variations:**
  * Teachers use text that features two simple sentences when first introducing the strategy (e.g. "Amina is going to school. She forgot her pen.") to help students learn the strategy.
  * Class revisits the text throughout the week to review new vocabulary
  * Teacher allow students to create a sentence they would add to the text (e.g. "She will borrow one from the teacher" or "She will return home to get it")
  * Class reads the text in different voices (e.g. deep voice, whisper voice, or loud voice)
* **Reflection Prompt:** How will this strategy help reluctant readers participate?
* **Teacher Voice:** "Echo and choral reading helped everyone participate, even shy learners and giving choice made independent reading feel safer."

#### READING_M2_STRAT4 / Strategy 4: Thinking Aloud

* **Description:** The teacher explains their thinking while reading.
* **Expanded Explanation:** Thinking aloud shows students what skilled readers do in their minds. This strategy is the teacher modeling positive ways students can think to themselves when trying to decode or understand a word. Thinking aloud makes the reading process visible and less mysterious for learners, especially those who struggle silently. When the teacher thinks aloud, it shows students that reading involves looking for clues, noticing letters, and checking meaning (e.g. "I see the first letter is n. I know n says /n/. This word could be 'net' and that makes sense because the story is about fishing."). Thinking aloud also shows students how good readers can make meaning from text, building comprehension skills. Teachers can model to students how to make predictions, summarize, and ask questions (e.g. "Hm, I wonder why the character did that? Maybe it was because…"). In crowded classrooms, it supports many students at once and is applicable for learners of all reading levels.
* **Examples / Variations:**
  * If reading from a book, the teacher can write words on the board so that students can see the text (e.g. "This word is spelled s-h-i-p." Teacher writes the word on the board. "This is a new word, but I can blend the sounds. Will you blend with me? /sh//i//p/ ship.")
  * Teacher models blending strategies, like chopping or shoulder-elbow-wrist, to figure out words
  * Teacher models how to connect the text to everyday life (e.g. "This reminds me of a time in life when…")
  * Teacher re-reads sentences where they paused to figure out a word (e.g "That word is ship. I will read this sentence again…")
  * Teacher uses a book or writes a simple text on the board as the print to model thinking aloud
  * Teacher invites a student volunteer to try leading thinking aloud
* **Reflection Prompt:** What is a statement you could say to model thinking aloud to your students?
* **Teacher Voice:** "I realized students didn't know how readers think." and "My students started using the thinking words I modeled."

#### READING_M2_STRAT5 / Strategy 5: Encourage Peer Discussion

* **Description:** Students briefly talk with a partner about what they read.
* **Expanded Explanation:** Peer discussion gives students a safe way to process what they read by talking with a partner. Turn-and-talk allows all learners to participate, even those who are hesitant to speak in whole class discussions. Teachers should give students guiding questions to prompt student thinking (e.g. "What was the order of events in the story?" or "What was the problem the main character faced? Was there a resolution?"). When students explain ideas to each other, they practice using new words and organizing their thoughts, which strengthens comprehension. Peer talk also builds connection and belonging by promoting reading as a shared experience rather than a test. Teachers should be active during turn-and-talks. They should be walking around the classroom, listening to discussions, and helping facilitate deeper questions for some pairs. Over time, students will become more confident expressing ideas and listening to others.
* **Examples / Variations:**
  * Teachers use the peer discussion as a way to make predictions before reading the story (e.g. "The title of our story is 'The Lost Shoe.' Turn-and-Talk about what you think may happen in our story") and before the resolution (e.g. "Abu wants to check one more place before giving up. Do you think he will find his shoe? Should he give up? Turn-and talk about what you think").
  * Teacher assigns weekly turn-and-talk partners to efficiently start peer discussions during activities. Teachers can rotate partners each week.
  * Teacher allows pairs to share their ideas with the whole class
  * Teachers instructs one partner to share first, then the teacher will call "change," this means the other partner will begin sharing
* **Reflection Prompt:** How can peer talk support student understanding of a story?
* **Teacher Voice:** "Students understood the story better after talking with a partner."
