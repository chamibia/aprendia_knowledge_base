### CON1 — Vocabulary Unlocks Meaning

Students may be able to decode words, but they do not know what they mean. Building a strong repertoire of vocabulary gives students access to the ideas, events, and emotions in a story. Introducing new, important vocabulary terms allows students to understand a text. Teaching vocabulary clearly through real-life examples and repetition allows students to add new words to their own spoken language. Students feel capable and engaged when they understand what they read.

### CON2 — Fluency Supports Comprehension

Fluency allows students to focus on meaning instead of decoding every word. When reading is slow or choppy, students can lose track of ideas, yet smooth reading allows students to think deeply about a text. Regular exposure to the same text helps students build familiarity, accuracy, expression, and vocabulary. Fluency grows through supported practice, instead of speed drills or memorization. As students increase in fluency, reading becomes more accessible and enjoyable.

### CON3 — Thoughtful Questions Deepen Thinking

Different types of questions guide students to think in different ways. Asking a variety of question types — literal, inference, prediction, deep, and personal — moves students beyond a surface level of understanding. When teachers vary their questions, students become more active and thoughtful readers. Thoughtful questions create interaction between students and the text itself. Students become critical thinkers and active readers instead of passive listeners.

### CON4 — Meaning Grows from Connection

Comprehension strengthens when students connect new readings to what they already know. Prior knowledge helps students make sense of and relate to new, unfamiliar topics. Allowing discussion in familiar languages can bridge understanding into the language of instruction. Connecting stories to students' lives makes reading experiences relevant and meaningful. When teachers value student experience, classrooms become inclusive and new ideas connect to familiar ones.

## STRATEGIES (Reference for Narrative)

> **Agent:** Use these when constructing the narrative. Do not deliver as separate content — embody them in the story scenes.

### READING_M4_STRAT1 — Teach New Words in Context (Vocabulary)

**Description:** Introduce new words using visuals, actions, and real-life examples.

**Expanded explanation:** Understanding words is essential for understanding text. When students decode a word, yet don't understand what it means, reading may feel confusing. Vocabulary development helps students connect spoken language and written language. Strong vocabulary supports comprehension and overall reading fluency. Teachers should introduce new words to students that are relevant and help children build meaning. When introducing new words, they should be explained clearly and through real-life examples (including objects or images, when possible). Using interactive activities that allow students to interact with vocabulary increases the likelihood that students will remember and use the new word. Vocabulary words should be used in the teacher and student spoken language in the classroom. Teaching a few new words at a time allows for students to learn terms in a meaningful way.

**Examples / Variations:**
- Teacher introduces new vocabulary through a story.
- Teacher chooses 2 to 4 new vocabulary words to review throughout each week.
- Teacher posts a word wall with new vocabulary.
- Class reviews new words during transitions.
- Class acts out new vocabulary words.
- Class creates sentences or stories using new vocabulary.

**Reflection prompt:** How can students show they understand a new word?

**Teacher Voice:** "When we review new words throughout the week, students begin using them in discussions."

---

### READING_M4_STRAT2 — Use Retelling to Check Understanding (Comprehension)

**Description:** Students retell stories in their own words to show what they understood.

**Expanded explanation:** Retelling helps students remember and organize ideas. Teachers should prompt students with simple sequencing words (e.g. first, next, then, after, last) to show clear transition between different events in a story or text. Retelling can be in various ways, like writing, drawing, acting, or orally. Retelling is an easy activity to bring choice into the classroom and allow students to choose which way they would like to retell a text to show understanding without pressure. Retelling also helps teachers identify confusion quickly. Teachers should analyze student retelling to check comprehension (e.g. Is there a part of the story a lot of students are having trouble retelling? Are students not retelling the beginning correctly — was there a word they did not understand?).

**Examples / Variations:**
- Some students act out the key events while other students narrate the actions.
- Students retell with a partner, then share with the whole class.
- Separate groups work together to retell specific events within the story (beginning, middle, end or rising action, climax, falling action, resolution).
- Student groups read different texts and have to teach other students about their text through retelling strategies.

**Reflection prompt:** Why might retelling be an important skill for life outside the classroom?

**Teacher Voice:** "Retelling helped me see which students are understanding and which need more support."

---

### READING_M4_STRAT3 — Build Fluency through Weekly Text Studies (Fluency)

**Description:** Students interact with a text multiple times during a week to build smooth, confident, expressive reading.

**Expanded explanation:** Fluency develops when students interact with and read the same text multiple times with clear purpose. A weekly text study allows students to move beyond decoding to understanding to reading with accuracy, smoothness, and expression. Weekly text studies should be brief, about 2 to 4 sentences long. Teachers should introduce the text on the first day of the school week, focusing on decoding the words and understanding any new vocabulary. As the week continues, each day, students interact with the text in a different way. Repeated interaction with one text creates structure and predictability, while supporting fluency and comprehension skills. Teachers can use strategies like echo and choral readings when first introducing the text at the beginning of the week and move to independent or group practice towards the end of the school week. The shift from supported to independent reading instills confidence in students that they are capable of tackling unfamiliar text, motivating them to read.

**Examples / Variations:**
- Teacher writes the text study on a poster and displays it during the whole week.
- Class uses finger tracking while reading the text.
- Teacher records the class reading on the first and last day with the text to allow them to hear their growth.
- Students draw a picture to show the meaning of the text, or act it out.
- Students work in small groups or pairs to practice reading the text.

**Reflection prompt:** How can weekly text studies reduce anxiety around reading?

**Teacher Voice:** "My students get excited each week when I introduce the new text study."

### READING_M4_STRAT4 — Ask Good Questions (Comprehension)

**Description:** Teachers ask a variety of questions to help students think and make connections.

**Expanded explanation:** Questions help guide student thinking and facilitate classroom discussion. Teachers who ask a variety of questions (literal, inference, prediction, deep, connection) support student critical thinking skills.
- Literal questions allow students to show direct understanding of a text (e.g. "What happened at the beginning of the story?" "Who were the main characters?").
- Inference questions allow students to apply what they learned during the text to reason why certain events or actions occurred (e.g. "Why did this happen?" "What do we know about our main character that supports their choice at the end of the story?").
- Prediction questions allow students to make a logical guess about what may happen or may happen next in a story (e.g. "After looking at the pictures, what do you think will happen in the story?" "What do you think will happen next?").
- Deep questions are typically open-ended and do not have one correct answer, yet they make students think critically about a text (e.g. "How would our story have changed, or not changed, if…?").
- Connection questions allow students to apply the text to their own lives and experiences (e.g. "How does this connect to you?"). Students should never be pressured to share answers to connection questions.

Asking varied questions makes learning interesting and builds curiosity. It is important for teachers to ask questions before, during, and after reading. This allows students to be engaged throughout the reading and allows students to notice how their initial ideas may change.

**Examples / Variations:**
- Teacher asks a deep and connection question together (e.g. "What is the lesson this story teaches? How does this relate to your own life?").
- Teacher allows students to share answers in small groups or pairs first, then some groups share with the whole class.
- Teacher invites students to ask a question to the whole class.
- Teacher asks students to predict how the story will end before reading, then after reading asks, "Was your prediction correct?"

**Reflection prompt:** What type of question do I ask students most?

**Teacher Voice:** "Changing my questions changed my students' thinking."

---

### READING_M4_STRAT5 — Use Background Knowledge

**Description:** Teachers connect reading to what students already know.

**Expanded explanation:** Children bring their own knowledge, experiences, and observations into the classroom. Students can use what they already know to make sense of new information. Teachers can support this by connecting readings to student experiences to activate background knowledge and help learner understanding (e.g. "Have you seen something like this before?" "Does this happen in our community?"). When students connect new words or text to what they already know, learning becomes deeper and fun. This strategy affirms student identities. Children who see their lives and experiences represented and valued during reading are more willing to take risks and share their thinking. Relevant texts allow students to become engaged readers.

**Examples / Variations:**
- Teacher uses locally relevant examples during reading, or asks students to share similar examples from their lives.
- Teacher brings in local objects/artifacts to connect reading to everyday life.
- Teacher invites students or members of the community to share a story from their tradition with the class (if appropriate).
- Teacher models how they relate personal experiences to classroom ideas and instruction.
- Teacher pauses during shared reading to ask connection questions (e.g. "Have you experienced something like this?").

**Reflection prompt:** How do I connect reading to students' experiences?

**Teacher Voice:** "My students understood more when we connected reading to their own lives."

### DIY_ACTIVITY_1: Clap and Retell

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **Materials** | Short story/text |
| **Steps** | 1. Teacher reads a short story aloud. / 2. Teacher reviews sequencing words with the class (e.g. first, next, then, after, finally). / 3. Students sit or stand in a circle. / 4. Teacher begins retelling the story, "First…", sharing one event. / 5. Teacher claps and calls a student's name. / 6. That student continues the retell with a sequencing word. / 7. Student claps and calls on another classmate. / 8. Continue until the story is retold. |
| **Variation (younger)** | Allow students to look at pictures while retelling. |
| **Variation (older)** | Students include specific details. |
| **Observation** | Students understand the order of events. |
| **Time** | 10 minutes |

### DIY_ACTIVITY_2: Same or Different?

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **Materials** | Short text |
| **Steps** | 1. Teacher reads a short text/story. / 2. While reading, the teacher asks, "Is this the same or different from your life?" / 3. Students turn and share with a partner in familiar language. / 4. Teacher invites students to share with the class in familiar language. / 5. Teacher helps translate words to terms students know in the language of instruction. / 6. Teacher connects student ideas back to the story. |
| **Variation (younger)** | Students use a thumbs up or thumbs down motion before sharing with a partner. |
| **Variation (older)** | Explain why the similarity or difference is important to the story. |
| **Observation** | Students participate more willingly. Students use their experience to understand the text. |
| **Time** | 15 minutes |
