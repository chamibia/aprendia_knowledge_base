### 2. Learning Objectives (2–3)

* Teachers implement peer support structures to strengthen relationships and support students learning from one another.

* Teachers connect learning to real-world experiences to increase participation and collaboration.

* Teachers can recognize and reinforce positive peer interactions to build a positive learning environment.

### 3. Teacher Motivations & Pain Points

* “It’s hard to get my students to work together. Some children are left out or ignored by their peers.”

* “I want my class to feel calm and helpful, but I am not sure how to guide peer support.”

* “I want students to support each other, not depend only on me.”

* “I spend a lot of time stopping conflicts between students.”

* “I have so many students in my class, I cannot always support everyone myself.”

### 4. Module Rules

* Peer interaction examples must emphasize safety, respect, and emotional regulation.  
* Do not include competitive, shaming, or exclusionary peer practices.  
* Strategies should encourage the teacher to model the behavior before asking students to do it independently.  
* Emphasize that teachers should move around the room and observe students during group or pair work.  
* Strategies should never suggest that students support each other at the expense of their own learning.  
* When introducing strategies for grouping and peer support, remind the user that peer interactions should never position one student as more powerful or smarter than the other. Peer support should encourage positive relationship building, collaboration, and shared learning.

### 5. Key Concepts

* **HC_M3_CON1 / Positive relationships reduce conflict.** Strong relationships help children feel safe, seen, and supported in the classroom. When students trust their teacher and peers, they are less likely to respond with anger, fear, or withdrawal. Students who experience conflict often experience disrupted relationships with caring adults. Restoring positive relationships can help children reestablish trust and security and can help prevent interpersonal conflict before it starts. When children feel connected to each other and their teachers, they are more motivated to listen, share, and resolve problems peacefully. 

* **HC_M3_CON2 / Structure promotes safe and supportive peer interactions.** Collaborative work is a key strategy in building positive relationships between students.  Children may want to work together, but feel unsure of how to do so respectfully. Clear roles, routines, and expectations give students guidance and reduce confusion during peer activities. Structure generates predictability for students; they know what is expected, which lowers anxiety and fear. In large or mixed-age classrooms, predictable peer routines foster inclusion and friendship while creating safe opportunities to learn and take risks. Peer interaction allows the classroom to become a place where students can learn from one another.

* **HC_M3_CON3 / Peer support builds confidence and belonging.** Helping others helps children to see themselves as capable and important. When students support peers, they develop empathy and stronger social connections. Peer support also helps students practice communication and problem-solving skills. Children are shaped by their experiences during the school day, so students, especially those in conflict settings, must be given opportunities to establish meaningful peer relationships to build a sense of belonging. In low-resource classrooms, peer support expands learning and emotional care beyond the teacher. 

* **HC_M3_CON4 / Real-world connection improves engagement.** Children are more engaged when lessons reflect their own experiences and environments. Familiar examples help students feel comfortable and reduce fear of making mistakes. When learning becomes applicable to their own lives, students understand why it matters. Classroom lessons and activities that reflect students’ lives invite participation and meaningful contributions. This approach is especially important for students who have experienced disruption. Relevant and emotionally safe learning helps students become active and responsive classroom participants. 

### **6\. Strategies**

#### **HC\_M3\_STRAT1 / Strategy 1: Structured Group Work**

* **Description:** Organize pairs or small groups with clear roles so students can work together collaboratively

* **Expanded Explanation:** Structured group work gives students a clear purpose and reduces confusion that can lead to conflict. Before allowing students to participate in group work, teachers should use interactive modeling to set expected behaviors, language, and interactions between students. Predictable group routines support emotional safety and cooperation. Assigning simple roles (like reader, cleaner, timekeeper, or writer) helps every child feel included and responsible. During group work, teachers must be active. Circulate around the classroom to offer support and monitor student behavior. 

* **Examples / Variations:** 

  * Write or use picture cards to display the different group roles

  * Keep the same groups for one week to build trust, then change groups the following week

  * Rotate student groups roles, allowing all students to try each role

  * Invite groups to self-check: “Did everyone get a turn to speak?”

  * Use pairs instead of small groups when space or time is limited

* **Reflection Prompt:** What group role could help quieter students participate more?

* **Quiz Item:** 

  * Why do clear roles matter in group work? (Options: reduce conflict / save time only / makes work harder)

  * Why should the teacher walk around the room during group work? (Keywords: monitoring behavior, reminding, support, encourage)

* **Teacher Voice:** “When I added roles, students argued less and stayed focused.”

#### HC_M3_STRAT2 / Strategy 2: Mixed-Ability Grouping

* **Description:** Group students so they can support each other’s learning.

* **Expanded Explanation:** Mixed-ability grouping encourages collaboration instead of comparison. Students learn that everyone has strengths and something to offer. Identify students in varied levels of understanding (e.g. above-grade level, on-grade level, below-grade level), based on teacher observations and/or assessments. Every group should include learners of all levels of understanding. Mixed groups should not shame any student and they should not be publicly told their understanding-level. The same rules and expectations for structured group work apply to mixed ability grouping. When students are working in mixed-ability groups, teachers may remind students to be supportive and help one another during the learning process. These groups should be small, around 2 to 4 students to ensure meaningful learning and participation.

* **Examples / Variations:** 

  * Pair students who use different strategies to solve/answer questions 

  * Rotate the groups on a regular basis (weekly or bi-weekly) to allow students learn from different peers

  * Use mixed-ability groups for activities, discussions, and games, then allow students to work independently

  * Students may change seating for some tasks, then return to their regular seats

* **Reflection Prompt:** In what ways does mixed grouping honor different strengths students bring to the classroom?

* **Quiz Item:** 

  * Why does mixed-ability grouping support learning? (Keywords: Peer support, belonging, confidence, different skills)

  * How can you form productive mixed groups? (Keywords: assessments, test scores, participation, observations)

* **Teacher Voice:** “When students worked in mixed groups, they started helping instead of comparing.”

#### **HC\_M3\_STRAT3 / Strategy 3: Turn-and-Talk**

* **Description:** Use short partner discussions so students can share ideas safely. 

* **Expanded Explanation:** Turn-and-talk gives every child the opportunity to speak without the pressure of speaking to the whole class. It helps students practice listening and respectful communication. Interactively model the rules and expectations for turn and talk, including staying on-task, voice level, and respectful language. Turn-and-talk is done in pairs. Teachers need to consider the number of students present to adjust if there may be one group of three. Teachers should clearly communicate the amount of time the class has to “turn-and talk” and who each student is paired with. Provide a question, statement, or topic for students to discuss with their partner.

* **Examples / Variations:** 

  * Invite a few pairs to share a summary of their discussion with the whole class

  * Pair students who are already sitting next to one another (no students have to move seats, making this a time efficient strategy)

  * Facilitate several rounds of turn-and-talk with the same question, allowing students to discuss with multiple classmates creating more discussion and idea-sharing

  * Use signals to represent when partner should switch sharing (clap once) and when the conversation should stop (clap twice)

* **Reflection Prompt:** When could a quick turn-and-talk help students feel heard?

* **Quiz Item:** 

  * Turn-and-talk gives every student a chance to participate. (Options: true/false)

  * Why does turn-and-talk help students feel safe sharing ideas? (Options: they talk to one peer first/only confident students speak/the teacher gives the answer)

* **Teacher Voice:** “Quiet students started sharing their thoughts when they talked to a partner”

#### HC_M3_STRAT4 / Strategy 4: Structured Peer Support

* **Description:** Teach students simple, respectful ways to help and speak to one another.

* **Expanded Explanation:** Peer interactions can build positive relationships and promote the learning process. Peer support allows students with low-understanding to receive help and allows high-understanding students to further learn through explaining/teaching. The whole class should learn the expectation for peer support, as all students are capable of helping others. Modeling phrases and behaviors reduces misunderstandings, conflict, and off-task behavior. Teachers should review asking a peer first before helping and using kind, respectful language. Reinforce how being a helpful peer relates back to overall classroom rules. Support can be academic, but also behavioral like turn-taking, listening, and using kind words.

* **Examples / Variations:** 

  * Models support phrases like “Can I help?” while monitoring student independent work

  * Challenge students to “Ask three before me.” If students need help, they must ask three classmates before asking the teacher for help.

  * Model how to use calming strategies when you feel frustrated that you do not understand (belly breathing) and how to ask a peer for help.

* **Reflection Prompt:** How can I model positive support for my students tomorrow?

* **Quiz Item:** 

  * Which phrase supports respectful peer help? (Options: “Let me do it.”/ “Can I show you my strategy?”/ “You are wrong.”)

* **Teacher Voice:** “When students explain ideas to each other, understanding improves.”

#### HC_M3_STRAT5 / Strategy 5: Connect Lessons to Children’s Lives**

* **Description:** Use familiar experiences to build trust and engagement.

* **Expanded Explanation:** When lessons reflect students’ daily lives, children feel seen and understood. Familiar examples lower anxiety and invite participation. Strategic questions prompt students to critically think about their own experiences (“What are some places you use math outside of school? Can you tell us how you used it?”). Using the five senses (smell, taste, touch, sight, hear) can help students recall their lived experiences  (“Today we are learning about fertilizer. When was the last time you went to a farm or gardened? What did it smell like?”). Roleplaying scenarios can also help students relate their own experiences. 

* **Examples / Variations:** 

  * Allow for students to share experiences in small groups, not just the whole class. 

  * When introducing a new topic, ask students to write or share what they already know and what they want to learn. 

  * Reference relevant local events, foods, or tasks to increase engagement 

  * If the teacher is aware of students who have background on a particular topic, ask them to prepare a related story to tell the class for the following day (e.g. One student has traveled to the capital city recently, they decided to share their experience using the bus system, since the class is learning about transportation).

* **Reflection Prompt:** What is a real-life example you could use in class this week?

* **Quiz Item:** 

  * What increases when lessons connect to students’ lives? (Keywords: participation, engagement, sharing, responsiveness)

  * Why do lessons connected to students’ lives increase participation? (Options: They are easier to grade / they take more time / they feel familiar and safe)

* **Teacher Voice:** “Students opened up more when lessons connected to their daily experience.”
