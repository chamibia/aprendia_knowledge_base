### 2. Learning Objectives (2–3)

* Teachers use locally available materials to create hands-on learning activities.

* Teachers ensure every child actively participates using simple objects.

* Teachers establish clear routines for materials to be used effectively and safely.

### 3. Teacher Motivations & Pain Points

* "I do not have any resources. I don't know how to make lessons active."

* "When I bring materials, children fight over who can use them."

* "My class is too big to give every student materials."

* "I try to play games, but my students become noisy and hard to manage."

### 4. Module Rules

* Focus on materials to support active learning, not general engagement strategies.
* Emphasize that hands-on materials can increase participation.
* Materials should not only be used for teaching, but also accessible for students to use.
* All resources and materials should be no-cost and made from local materials.
* Using materials is a core part of active learning — ensure that examples are not redundant or repetitive.

### 5. Key Concepts

* **AIL_M4_CON1 / Materials are everywhere.** Even in low-resource contexts, useful learning materials are all around — like sticks, sand, stones, and fabric. Using familiar objects connects learning to children's real lives and makes lessons feel meaningful. Teachers do not need to spend money to make learning hands-on.

* **AIL_M4_CON2 / Active Participation Supports Wellbeing.** When every child is engaged, fewer children feel left out or bored. Active participation can reduce behavior challenges, helping children feel included and capable. Hands-on learning makes students excited to attend school and participate in learning. In stressful environments, this involvement supports emotional safety and belonging.

* **AIL_M4_CON3 / Structure Makes Hands-On Learning Work.** Without clear routines and expectations, materials can cause noise, conflict, or wasted time. Simple systems for sharing, using, and collecting materials create predictability. Learning how to use materials efficiently helps students develop responsibility and creates a more autonomous classroom.

* **AIL_M4_CON4 / Learning is Stronger When Students Can Touch and Do.** Students' understanding of content can become deeper when they handle objects, not just listen to instruction. Hands-on learning helps children focus, reduces frustration, and builds confidence through action. Tactile experiences allow students to develop curiosity for the learning process and build problem-solving skills.

### 6. Strategies

#### AIL_M4_STRAT1 / Strategy 1: Find and Collect Materials Around You

* **Description:** Use free, local objects as learning tools by regularly collecting and preparing them for lessons.

* **Expanded Explanation:** Materials do not need to be expensive to be effective. Collecting items like sticks, stones, bottle caps, seeds, fabric, or paper gives teachers a ready supply of tools for hands-on learning across different subjects. Involving children and/or the community in collecting materials builds ownership and responsibility while reducing pressure on teachers to prepare everything. Making it a habit to collect local and familiar resources makes teaching hands-on lessons easy and low-stress. Teachers can find a safe place in their classroom or school to store collected materials. Teachers should ensure that materials are safe for students to use by checking for sharp edges, splinters, and other hazards.

* **Examples / Variations:**

  * Teacher asks students to bring in five small stones each for a counting lesson.

  * Teacher stores class materials in an empty carton from the local shop.

  * Each student brings a clean plastic bottle from home to use for a math game.

  * Teacher splits the class into groups and each group is responsible for collecting different materials (e.g. sticks, stones, seeds).

  * Teacher picks up different seeds on their evening walk that they can use for a blending activity.

* **Reflection Prompt:** What materials exist around your classroom that you can start collecting today?

* **Quiz Item:**

  * Which is an example of a local learning material? (Options: Fabric / Computers / Textbooks)

  * How can collecting materials reduce stress for teachers? (Keywords: no cost, always available, prepared)

* **Teacher Voice:** "I did not realize I have free resources around me."

#### AIL_M4_STRAT2 / Strategy 2: Give Every Child a Material to Hold

* **Description:** Give each student an object to use to actively participate.

* **Expanded Explanation:** When only a few students handle materials, others may become passive or distracted. Giving every child something to interact with keeps them focused and involved in learning. This reduces waiting time, prevents conflict, and increases active learning opportunities. Only giving some students resources can make the classroom community feel separated and disconnected. For some resources, it may not be possible for teachers to make a class set (e.g. blending mats, posters, or models). In those cases, teachers can place students in small groups and rotate the resource around the classroom. During lesson planning, teachers can ask themselves whether an activity can allow all students to use local resources — if so, they can prepare for each child to have their own.

* **Examples / Variations:**

  * Teacher gives each student three seeds to touch the sounds in simple words.

  * Teacher gives each student sticks to create simple shapes.

  * Teacher creates one poster, but students come in small groups to play a quick game with it while others work independently.

  * Students work in pairs to organize 10 stones to represent as many math equations as possible.

  * Each student has a stone and a stick — if they agree with an answer they hold up the stone, and if they disagree they hold up the stick.

* **Reflection Prompt:** How can you make sure every child participates in your next lesson?

* **Quiz Item:** What is one benefit of giving every child a material to hold? (Options: It makes the class quieter / It increases participation / It reduces lesson time)

* **Teacher Voice:** "My students stay more focused when everyone has a resource."

#### AIL_M4_STRAT3 / Strategy 3: Connect the Materials to the Learning Objective

* **Description:** Choose materials that clearly help students understand the learning goal.

* **Expanded Explanation:** Materials are used to support learning, not distract from it. When objects clearly represent the learning objective, children can see and connect ideas more easily. Teachers can explain how resources are tools that help us understand. Resources can help students experiment and make mistakes. Resources allow students to learn through discovery, not to play without purpose. Materials can also support making concepts more concrete and familiar. When teachers use familiar materials (e.g. a watering can and seeds to introduce gardening as a science topic), students can connect new ideas to things they already know from their everyday experiences. This bridges the classroom and the real world, making learning memorable and relevant.

* **Examples / Variations:**

  * If the learning goal is "I can identify shapes," materials can be used to match a picture of a shape to the correct name card.

  * If the learning goal is "I can divide numbers by three," materials can be sticks.

  * If the learning goal is "I can describe the members of my family," materials can be sentence frames or picture cards with family vocabulary words.

* **Reflection Prompt:** What material could you use to make your next learning objective more concrete for students?

* **Quiz Item:** What should guide your choice of materials for a lesson? (Options: What looks the most fun / What connects to the learning objective / What is easiest to collect)

* **Teacher Voice:** "Materials have to match the lesson to actually help students learn."

#### AIL_M4_STRAT4 / Strategy 4: Integrate Games into Learning

* **Description:** Use simple, low-resource games to practice skills.

* **Expanded Explanation:** Games turn practice into active and enjoyable learning. Structured games help students repeat skills while staying motivated and engaged. In stressful environments, games bring energy and joy while still supporting learning goals. Teachers facilitate games to encourage participation and reduce fear of making mistakes. Games like matching, sorting, question tosses, and relay challenges can be adapted to reflect literacy, numeracy, or social-emotional skills. Teachers can note the types of games students enjoy and adapt them to fit other content areas. A game is not a break from learning — it is a structured, active way to practice and consolidate what children are learning.

* **Examples / Variations:**

  * Students play a matching game. Each student has a card with a capital or small letter, students walk around the room holding the card to their chest while the teacher plays music — when the music stops, students find the person with their matching letter.

  * Teacher provides small groups with bottle caps that have numbers written on them. Students sort the bottle caps into different groups (e.g. odd, even, multiples of 4).

  * The class stands in a circle. The teacher asks a question about the shared text and when a student raises their hand, the teacher tosses a paper ball to them. The teacher repeats and can let students ask questions too.

  * The teacher splits the class into two teams and writes the same incorrect sentence on the board. Each team sends one student up at a time to correct the sentence, but each student can only fix one thing. The team that corrects the entire sentence first wins.

* **Reflection Prompt:** What is a simple game you could add to your next math lesson?

* **Quiz Item:** How do games support learning, not just fun? (Keywords: practice, repeat skills, engagement, motivation)

* **Teacher Voice:** "My students become excited to learn when we play a game."

#### AIL_M4_STRAT5 / Strategy 5: Establish Fast, Fair Material Routines

* **Description:** Create simple systems for giving out, using, and collecting materials efficiently.

* **Expanded Explanation:** Without routines, materials can cause confusion, noise, and lost learning time. Clear steps help children know exactly what to do, reducing stress and conflict. Assigning roles builds responsibility and keeps the classroom organized. Teachers can use interactive modeling to establish expectations with students and practice what material routines should look like — including passing out, using, and collecting materials. When students understand what is expected of them, they are able to meet those expectations. Teachers can also create classroom jobs related to material routines (e.g. material sharer and material collector).

* **Examples / Variations:**

  * Teacher uses signals (e.g. a clap or short phrase) to start and stop activities.

  * Teacher assigns a timekeeper to alert the class when it is time to begin collecting materials and transitioning to the next activity.

  * Teacher assigns a materials manager each week to organize materials at the end of the day.

  * Each table group or small group has their own carton to collect their materials in.

  * Students count the number of bottle caps they were given to make sure they collect them all at the end of the activity.

  * Students practice using a voice level appropriate for group work.

* **Reflection Prompt:** What routine could save your class time when using materials?

* **Quiz Item:** Why are routines important when using materials in a large class? (Keywords: calm, predictable, safe learning, reduce confusion, clear expectations)

* **Teacher Voice:** "Setting expectations and having a routine makes using materials calmer and faster."
