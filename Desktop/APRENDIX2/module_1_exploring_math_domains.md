## LEARNING OBJECTIVES

- Teachers understand the five domains of math (number sense, operations, measurement & data, geometry, reasoning/problem solving)
- Teachers can connect domains to daily classroom and community practices, selecting low-cost materials and quick routines that fit their context
- Teachers recognize math as a journey and prioritize sense-making over speed or memorization

---

## MODULE RULES

- Always frame math as sense-making, not speed or memorization
- When introducing any domain, anchor it in a familiar daily routine
- When introducing Concept 5 (Reasoning & Problem Solving), always include that it is not a domain of its own, but a set of skills that cuts across all other math domains
- Use "you already do this" language to build confidence, where appropriate
- Reinforce coherence—emphasize that domains work together

## CONCEPTS

### MATH_M1_CON1 — Number Sense
📷 Send image with caption, then deliver concept content:
https://i.imgur.com/pNWEBrS.jpeg
Number Sense — understanding what numbers mean, how they relate to each other, and how they represent quantity, order, and comparison.
**Definition:** Understanding what numbers mean, how they relate to each other, and how they can represent quantity, order, and comparison.

**Expanded explanation:** Children use number sense every day. For example, to know how many cups are needed for lunch or how many students are in class. In emergencies and low-resource settings, building number sense contributes to stable routines (e.g., counting in lines, sharing materials) that reduce anxiety and create predictability. It helps children attach meaning to quantities they meet daily (e.g., days until school meets again, steps between home and school), so math feels useful, not abstract. Strong number sense also supports quick checks for fairness and safety (e.g., "Do we have enough for everyone?"), which builds confidence and trust in the classroom.

**Examples (choose 1):**
- Count with stones or bottle caps (individuals, pairs, groups)
- Compare more/less/same between two collections
- Body counting: count with hands/feet, claps, steps
- Bundle sticks/caps into groups of 10 → trade 10 ones for 1 "ten"

**Reflection prompt:** What is one daily routine where your students already practice counting?

**Quiz item:** What does number sense mean? (Keywords: quantity, order, comparison)

### MATH_M1_CON2 — Operations
📷 Send image with caption, then deliver concept content:
https://i.imgur.com/hCCQtEC.jpeg
Operations — addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division show up in everyday classroom moments.
**Definition:** Performing basic calculations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division) and understanding what they mean and when to use them.

**Expanded explanation:** Operations are actions on quantities: join (add), separate (subtract), combine equal groups (multiply), and share/partition (divide). Addition and subtraction show up in attendance ("How many students are here? How many are absent?"), in sharing food ("If we have 12 crackers for 6 students, how many does each student get?"), and in keeping inventory ("We had 10 crayons but 3 broke. How many do we have left?"). Multiplication and division appear naturally in moments like grouping for games or tasks, or estimating materials needs ("Each student needs 2 stones for this activity. How many do we need total?"). When teaching operations, encourage students to explain not just the answer, but the situation. This keeps practice active, visible, and meaningful.

**Examples (choose 1):**
- Share 8 mangoes among 4 children and discuss "fair sharing"
- Separate a group of items in different ways and record
- Show equal jumps on a ground number line (e.g., +3, +3, +3)

**Reflection prompt:** What real-life activity could you use to practice addition?

**Quiz item:** Give one example of a real-life division activity. (Keywords: share, split, divide, fair, equal, groups, distribute)

### MATH_M1_CON3 — Measurement & Data

**Definition:** Comparing and quantifying objects by length, weight, capacity, or time; collecting, organizing, and interpreting simple data.

**Expanded explanation:** Measurement supports everyday skills related to eating, managing time, sharing, and judging distances. Use nonstandard tools first (string, hand spans, cups) before rulers/scales to keep learning accessible and related to real life. Simple data (tallies, pictographs) help children make decisions and compare information. Keep tasks hands-on, visible, and quick so all learners can participate even without textbooks.

**Examples (choose 1):**
- Measure classroom length with rope or hand spans
- Tally daily attendance and discuss more/less than yesterday
- Compare full/half/empty containers to discuss capacity
- Create a class pictograph or bar chart (e.g., favorite fruits/games)

**Reflection prompt:** What object in your classroom could be used to compare length?

**Quiz item:** Measurement helps students connect math to what? (Keywords: world, compare, quantify)

### MATH_M1_CON4 — Geometry

**Definition:** Recognizing, naming, and describing shapes; understanding spatial relationships like direction, position, and symmetry.

**Expanded explanation:** Geometry links to children's environment: rectangles in classroom walls and doors, circles in bowls and buckets, triangles in roof frames, etc. Spatial language helps with navigation and safety: left/right along paths, behind/in front of during safety drills, inside/outside when discussing safe areas to play, etc. Identifying shapes, using geometric vocabulary, and observation skills help students describe and make sense of the world around them.

**Examples (choose 1):**
- Identify rectangles and circles around the classroom/schoolyard
- Trace or outline shapes on the ground with chalk or sticks
- Draw triangles in dirt/sand, count sides and corners
- Fold paper to find and mark lines of symmetry

**Reflection prompt:** What shapes do your students already see daily?

**Quiz item:** Geometry helps students understand what? (Keywords: space, shape, position)

### MATH_M1_CON5 — Mathematical Reasoning & Problem Solving
📷 Send image with caption, then deliver concept content:
https://i.imgur.com/ro78Cwn.jpeg
Mathematical Reasoning cuts across all math domains — it's how students explain their thinking and make sense of problems.

> **[CRITICAL: State that this cuts across ALL domains, not a separate domain]**

**Definition:** Using logical thinking to make decisions, explain answers, and solve problems.

**Expanded explanation:** Mathematical reasoning and problem solving skills cut across all math domains. Reasoning is the bridge between answers and understanding. Particularly in emergencies, children must make decisions carefully, considering safety, fairness, cultural norms, etc. A sentence frame like claim-because-check (e.g., "I think each student gets 2 stones because 12/6 is 2. Let's check with stones.") can help children learn about justifying their choices and build resilience to correct themselves if needed. Teachers can encourage multiple strategies (e.g., drawing, acting, using objects) to solve a problem, then ask questions like "Why does this work?" or "Could there be another way?" Over time, children learn that at its core math is about sense-making, which supports fairness, safety, and good decisions.

**Examples (choose 1):**
- Use "because…" sentences to justify a solution
- Act out a story problem, then model and check with objects
- Compare two strategies on the board, vote which is clearer, and explain why
- Quick reasoning checks (e.g., "too small / about right / too big" line on the floor)

**Reflection prompt:** How can you ask your students to explain their answers?

**Quiz item:** What is math reasoning? (Keywords: explain, logic, thinking)

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **Time** | 10-15 minutes |
| **Materials** | 2 containers of different sizes, water, chalkboard, chalk |
| **Steps** | 1. Show both containers / 2. Ask students to estimate how many cups fill the larger container—thumbs up = more than 5, sideways = about 5, down = fewer than 5 / 3.
