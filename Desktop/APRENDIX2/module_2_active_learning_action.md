### AIL_M2_CON1 — What Active Learning Is and Isn't

Active learning is student-centered teaching where children engage directly with content through guided activities, interactions with peers or materials, and opportunities to question, experiment, and discover. It is not unstructured free time — it is a purposeful method for delivering the curriculum children are already learning.

### AIL_M2_CON2 — Why Active Learning Matters

Children's brains learn best when they are actively doing, not passively listening. In crisis-affected settings especially, active learning deepens understanding while also supporting children's wellbeing and resilience. When children feel engaged and safe, they are better able to learn.

### AIL_M2_CON3 — Active Learning is Possible Anywhere

Active learning does not require special materials or a perfect classroom. Even in crowded or low-resource environments, teachers can use simple strategies like peer discussion or local objects to make learning more meaningful. Learning can be an inclusive, shared experience even when resources are limited.

### AIL_M2_CON4 — Active Learning Builds Safety and Inclusion

Active learning creates opportunities for every child to participate. This is especially important in large or mixed-age classrooms where some students may stay quiet or be overlooked. When teachers encourage participation in different ways, it builds student confidence, belonging, and sense of community.

## STRATEGIES (User-facing)

### AIL_M2_STRAT1 — Make Inclusion the Foundation of Active Learning

**Description:** Every child participates and engages in learning, regardless of ability, language, background, or learning needs.

**Expanded explanation:** Active learning is most effective when every child has meaningful opportunities to participate, explore, and learn. Inclusion is not separate from lessons or activities — it is a daily teaching practice and a foundation for every active learning experience. When teachers plan active learning lessons, they should think intentionally about how every child will engage with content and participate in the learning process. Inclusion allows every student to be part of learning and values each child's unique way of thinking and contributing to the classroom. When students are included, they feel valued and safe in the classroom environment. They understand that their ideas matter and that participation does not require perfection. When inclusion becomes part of everyday classroom practice, students are more prepared and confident to ask questions, take risks, and engage in lessons. Inclusion makes learning accessible, meeting students where they are while still challenging them to think deeply and grow as learners.

**Examples / Variations:**
- While lesson planning, the teacher identifies which students may need help with language, need movement, or need extra teacher support, and plans accordingly.
- Teacher uses peer work for students to collaborate and learn from one another.
- Teacher offers different movement variations during lessons (e.g. "You can choose to stand up or raise both hands in the air to show that you think the sentence is written correctly.").
- Teacher models respectful and supportive language to students (e.g. "Good work! You solved this equation in a way I did not think of." or "This question was difficult. Can I show you my strategy?").
- Teacher and students create classroom rules that emphasize being kind and helpful to one another (e.g. Be respectful, Treat others with kindness, Be a good friend, Be safe).

**Reflection prompt:** Think of students in your classroom who observe more than they participate — what might be a barrier?

**Teacher Voice:** "When I started planning for all learners, I noticed that more students were participating and enjoying class."

### AIL_M2_STRAT2 — Find the Active Learning Opportunities

**Description:** Consider how each part of a lesson can use active learning.

**Expanded explanation:** Teachers may feel like they need to completely change their lessons to make them active, which can feel overwhelming. Instead, they can keep their lesson and look for small moments where children can be active instead of passive learners. Small changes can help students stay involved, deepen their learning, and reduce off-task behavior. In crisis contexts, even brief moments of participation can help children feel more connected and less withdrawn. While lesson planning, teachers can ask themselves simple questions like: Where could children interact with a material? Where could they work with a peer? Where could they move? A lesson does not have to include all of these ways to create active learning, but including at least one can make a difference in student learning outcomes.

**Examples / Variations:**
- Students stay seated to agree or stand to disagree with different sentences about a character from the class story.
- Students practice writing letters in sand.
- Teacher splits students into small groups and gives each group a different math problem to represent with pictures.
- Teacher has students turn and talk to someone next to them to share a connection they made between a story and their own lives.

**Reflection prompt:** What is one moment in your next lesson where you can pause and involve students more?

**Teacher Voice:** "Thinking about active learning during lesson planning makes my teaching more engaging."

### AIL_M2_STRAT3 — Use Movement to Reinforce Learning

**Description:** Students use their bodies to engage with the learning content.

**Expanded explanation:** When children sit for long periods of time, it can lead to restlessness or loss of focus. Movement gives children a chance to release energy while still learning and staying engaged with the content. Movement can help introduce learning, deepen understanding, or give students a way to show what they know. Acting out ideas or responding with their bodies makes abstract ideas easier to understand and remember. This is especially helpful for younger learners or those still developing language skills. In stressful or crisis contexts, movement can bring joy and energy into the classroom. Movement activities should be inclusive and adaptable for all learners, including children with mobility challenges or disabilities. Teachers can use inclusive movements like pointing, clapping, facial expressions, or seated movements. Whenever using movement in the classroom, teachers should review how to move safely and in consideration of others.

**Examples / Variations:**
- Students act out a story instead of writing a summary.
- A few students act out vocabulary words while the class guesses.
- Teacher leads a walk around the school grounds to identify shapes in nature.
- Teacher creates a pattern with claps and stomps, then students must recreate it.
- Students use facial expressions to reflect how the main character is feeling at different points in the story.

**Reflection prompt:** When during the school day do your students need a simple movement activity?

**Teacher Voice:** "I worry about losing control of my class, but simple movements do not have to disrupt learning."

### AIL_M2_STRAT4 — Use Everyday Materials to Make Learning Concrete

**Description:** Simple objects in the local or school environment can transform a lesson.

**Expanded explanation:** Simple items like stones, sticks, or bottle caps allow students to see and touch what they are learning. Learning materials help make ideas real and tangible for students and help them reach learning objectives. When students have opportunities to learn with the support of local materials, their minds become more active and focused. Using their hands to sort, build, move, or explore objects helps students feel a sense of discovery, curiosity, and ownership over their learning. In large classes, giving each child, pair, or small group something to use and feel keeps them involved in the lesson; this can help students focus their energy and collaborate with peers. Children with different learning needs may especially benefit from seeing, touching, sorting, or building objects while learning. In crisis settings, using familiar local materials can also help children feel grounded and connected to their environment. Teachers do not need expensive resources to create meaningful learning experiences — simple everyday objects can support creativity, participation, and problem-solving skills.

**Examples / Variations:**
- Students use stones for counting and grouping.
- Students sort bottle caps by size and color.
- Students use sticks to create models of 3-dimensional shapes.
- Teacher uses sticks to help students understand place value.

**Reflection prompt:** What materials around your classroom or community could you use in a lesson this week?

**Teacher Voice:** "Using materials helps my students who struggle to understand just by listening."

---

### AIL_M2_STRAT5 — Use Peer Interaction to Deepen Learning

**Description:** Peer interaction gives every child a chance to speak, think, and practice ideas in a low-pressure way.

**Expanded explanation:** When students only listen to the teacher, many may not fully understand — but peer interaction allows students to talk through ideas, ask questions, and learn from each other. These interactions should be short, structured, and purposeful. A quick turn-and-talk or small group share can help ensure all students are included in the learning process. In crisis-affected or large classrooms, peer interaction also helps build relationships, trust, and a sense of belonging. Even brief moments of partner discussion can increase confidence and understanding. While peer interaction is occurring, teachers should move around the learning space and listen to student conversations. Teachers should set expectations for efficient peer work (e.g. respectful language, active listening, appropriate voice level).

**Examples / Variations:**
- Teacher uses mixed-age groups for students to discuss a new topic in social studies.
- Students at the same learning level work in small groups to solve a math word problem.
- Students work in pairs to draw their daily routine at school.

**Reflection prompt:** How could peer interactions help your quieter students?

**Teacher Voice:** "Some students never speak in front of the class, but will share ideas in small groups."

### DIY_ACTIVITY_1: Build the Story

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **Time** | ~10 minutes |
| **Materials** | Stones, sticks, bottle caps, or local materials |
| **Steps** | 1. Share a story with the class / 2. Give small groups a set of local objects / 3. Assign each group a scene, setting, or character from the story to build / 4. Ask students to explain what they created / 5. Invite a few groups to share with the class |
| **Variation (younger)** | Give groups simple scenes to depict (e.g. beginning, middle, end) |
| **Variation (older)** | Add something to the scene or character that would enhance the story |
| **Observation** | Students demonstrate understanding through hands-on learning |

### DIY_ACTIVITY_2: Guess the Word

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **Time** | ~10 minutes |
| **Materials** | Bowl and paper |
| **Steps** | 1. Write vocabulary words on small pieces of paper / 2. Place papers in a bowl / 3. Allow students to volunteer, choose a paper, and act out the word / 4. The class watches and guesses the vocabulary word |
| **Variation (younger)** | Teacher reads the word to the volunteer in the local language |
| **Variation (older)** | After guessing, students must use the word in a sentence |
| **Observation** | Students show understanding through movement and creativity |
