### 2. Learning Objectives (2–3)

* Teachers connect spoken language to letters and sounds, building student phonemic awareness and phonics skills.
* Teachers model blending and segmenting to help students learn and practice decoding skills.
* Teachers use simple routines and manipulatives to practice reading skills in an engaging and interactive way.

### 3. Module Rules

* Encourage teachers using modeling skills
* When introducing sound awareness/phonological awareness activities remind teachers that these activities do not involve text - they are focused on what sounds children hear, not how the letters/words are written.
* Teachers should include their students throughout instruction, facilitating conversations, and asking questions to assess and advance student knowledge and connections

### 4. Key Concepts

* **READING_M3_CON1 / Hearing sounds comes before reading words**. Before children can read words, they must be able to hear the sounds inside spoken language. This includes noticing rhyme, syllables, and the beginning, medial, and final sounds in words they use or hear everyday. These activities do not require any text - they can even be done with eyes closed. When students can hear and play with sounds, they begin to understand that words are made up of different parts. Auditory processing skills can be practiced through oral activities. When sound awareness is established, reading feels possible instead of overwhelming.

* **READING_M3_CON2 / Decoding builds independence**. Reading becomes powerful when students can decode words instead of memorizing them. Sounding out teaches students to move step by step, letter to sound to word. Decoding gives students a strategy they can use as they continue to grow as readers. This is especially important in low-resource classrooms where exposure to print may be limited. When students know how to sound out words, they begin to see themselves as capable readers.

* **READING_M3_CON3 / Print follows rules**. To understand the predictable patterns and mechanics of print, students need to interact with text directly. Students will use text throughout their lives. Knowing where to begin reading, which direction text moves, and how spacing works are essential concepts for students to understand print and feel confident interacting with it. Clear routines and instruction about print makes reading less confusing. When teachers discuss print during lessons or read alouds, students can gain language for how reading works.

* **READING_M3_CON4 / Reading skills grow when practiced together.** Strong reading develops when phonemic awareness, phonics, and print concepts are practiced together. Teaching skills in isolation can make reading feel disconnected. An integrated approach allows students to recognize how different skills fit together to make reading easier. Using manipulatives to practice blending and segmenting, along with word building, allows students to make connections through active learning. Practicing reading skills together sets students up for success.

### 5. Strategies

#### READING_M3_STRAT1 / Strategy 1: Help Children Hear Sounds in Language (Phonological Awareness)

* **Description:** Children practice listening to and playing with sounds in spoken words
* **Expanded Explanation:** Phonological awareness helps children recognize and play with sounds in spoken language. Activities like rhyming, clapping syllables, and identifying beginning sounds build phonological awareness. These activities are done orally without printed materials and are accessible to low-resource classrooms. Teachers should speak clearly and slowly when leading activities to help children hear sounds in language. Using hand motions, or small actions, helps learners recognize the sounds the teacher is emphasizing. For each activity, teachers should be giving multiple examples, about four or five, so that students can practice and gain understanding of the particular sound skill. When first introducing an activity, the teacher should model how students respond. Once students are familiar with sound activities, the teacher should give a brief wait time to allow students to respond. Hearing sounds clearly prepares students to connect sounds to letters later.
* **Examples / Variations:**
  * Clap the syllables in students' names (e.g. "My name is Bintou, Bin (clap)-tou (clap), two syllables")
  * Pick the word that starts with the given beginning sound (e.g. "Which word starts with /h/? jump, hop. Yes, hop begins with /h/. Get, have? Yes, it begins with /h/…")
  * Identify words as rhyming or not rhyming (e.g "Flip, sip… Yes, flip and sip rhyme. Jet, win… You are right! Jet and win do not rhyme. What is a word that could rhyme with jet?")
  * Name the beginning sound (e.g. "What is the beginning sound of these words? Sock, sip, summer. Yes, /s/ is the beginning sound. Dirt, dog, dent. Yes, /d/ is the beginning sound.")
  * Repeat a rhyme sentence with echo and choral reading (e.g. "The goat wears a coat on a boat.")
  * Teacher places hands face up slightly apart, lifts left hand says /m/, lifts right hand says /et/, brings hands together says "met," "What was the first sound?... Yes, it was /m/")
* **Reflection Prompt:** What is a sound game that could help your students?
* **Teacher Voice:** "Sound games helped my quieter students join in."

#### READING_M3_STRAT2 / Strategy 2: Connect Sounds to Letters (Phonics)

* **Description:** Teach which letters make which sounds
* **Expanded Explanation:** Phonics connects spoken sounds to written letters. Teachers present a letter and its sound clearly and repeatedly (e.g. "This is the letter L. L says /l/. L says /l/.") Teachers can write the letter on the boards, slate, poster, or letter card. After a letter and its sound are presented, the teacher has the students practice saying the sounds using echo and choral reading (e.g. "L say /l/. L says /l/.") Teachers should listen carefully while students are saying letter sounds, ensuring that students are saying it accurately and correcting the pronunciation if not. Returning to the same sounds often helps students decode, reducing guessing and memorization. Consistent, daily phonics instruction supports strong reading skills. Teachers know their class best, but working with two new letter sounds per week is a manageable pace for most learners. It allows students to play with the sounds through repetition and making connections.
* **Examples / Variations:**
  * Teacher asks students to name things or words that start with the given letter/sound (e.g. "What words or things do you know that start with g /g/? Yes, goat starts with g, /g/ goat… Yes, give starts with g, /g/ give.")
  * Teacher presents a classroom object that begins with the given letter/sound (e.g. Teacher holds a pen and writes P on the board. "What is this?... Yes, a pen. We know p says….?")
  * Students practicing writing the letter and saying the sound on a slate or notebook
  * Students draw a picture of an object that starts with a given letter or write a word the begins with a given letter
  * Teacher reviews previously learned letters and sounds before introducing a new sound
* **Reflection Prompt:** Which letter sounds might be most challenging for students?
* **Teacher Voice:** "Reviewing letter sounds every day helped my students stop guessing."

#### READING_M3_STRAT3 / Strategy 3: Model Blending and Segmenting (Phonics)

* **Description:** Teacher models breaking words into sound and blending them back together.
* **Expanded Explanation:** As students learn individual letter sounds, they should also be learning how to use those sounds to read words. Teachers model blending by pointing to each letter and stretching the sound slowly. Then they read the whole word (e.g. "/m/…/a/…/p/… map"). This helps students see decoding and understand that reading is not guessing. Segmenting works in the reverse order. The teacher says the whole word and then breaks it into sounds (e.g. "map… /m/ /a/ /p/… map"). This helps students understand how words can be broken down or taken apart. Modeling both blending and segmenting helps students read new words and supports early spelling. Also, it teaches students it is okay to take their time when reading. Teachers should invite students to blend and segment with them as practice for when they are reading alone. The decoding skills formed by blending and segmenting helps students learn how to figure out a word they may not know.
* **Examples / Variations:**
  * Teacher uses letter cards and a blending squares to blend and segment, allowing students to tap each letter sound (e.g. tap… /l/…tap…/o/…tap…/t/…slide finger across letters "lot")
  * Teacher works with small groups of students of similar ability to check understanding and reteach sounds
  * Teacher works with small groups of students of mixed ability to allow students to help teach and learn from each other
  * Teacher uses objects, drawings, or picture cards to create words from those objects (e.g. Teacher draws a cat. "What is this animal called? Yes, it is a cat. Let's break the word into sounds…" )
* **Reflection Prompt:** Do my students try breaking down words they do not know? Why or why not?
* **Teacher Voice:** "When I slowed down and blended out loud, students saw how reading works."

#### READING_M3_STRAT4 / Strategy 4: Teach How Print Works (Print Concepts, Oral Language)

* **Description:** Explain how print works so students recognize the patterns of text
* **Expanded Explanation:** For students to become proficient readers, they need to learn the mechanics of print. Teachers can name and point out parts of print while reading books or written text, like the title and spacing (e.g. "What do you notice in between each word?... Yes there is a space. Spaces separate words. Would it be hard to read without spaces? Why?"/ "Before reading, we should check if the text has a title. Where do you think a reader can find a title?..."). They also should draw specific attention to where text starts and the direction print moves. This will help students track accurately and support writing skills. Simple cues like, "We start reading here" or "These are the words we read," draw student attention to important details of print. When print routines are clear and consistent, students feel less confused and more confident using their decoding skills.
* **Examples / Variations:**
  * Teacher writes short stories on the board or poster (3 to 5 sentences) that feature a title, clear spacing, and punctuation
  * Teacher models finger tracking while reading and blending unknown words
  * In small groups students review a book or written story on piece of paper and identify title, spacing, and direction of text
  * Teacher invites students to consider how pictures can support the text or tell readers more about the story (e.g. "Does the picture match the words we read? What do you notice is happening in the picture?")
  * Students create their own books or short stories with title, spacing, and clear text direction
* **Reflection Prompt:** How often do I point out print concepts to students?
* **Teacher Voice:** "I didn't realize some students didn't know where to start reading."

#### READING_M3_STRAT5 / Strategy 5: Word Building with Manipulatives

* **Description:** Students build and change words using simple letter materials to practice decoding.
* **Expanded Explanation:** Word building allows students to use the letter-sound knowledge they have learned in a hands-on way. Students can use letter cards, bottle caps, paper, cardboard, or stones to build words by placing letters together then blending the sounds. Changing one letter at a time helps students see how small changes affect a whole word (e.g. changing the beginning, medial, or final letter - "pat" to "pet" to "let" to "led"). This supports decoding because students are actively blending sounds of randomized words or non-words. Part of learning how to read is noticing if a word sounds correct or not. Teachers should use non-words, also called nonsense words, to help students critically think about how words sound and if they have heard them in spoken language (e.g. "Watch how I change 'led' to 'med.' Is 'med' a real word or a non-sense word?"). Manipulatives can be helpful for learners to create tactile experiences for abstract concepts. Word building turns reading into problem solving, reducing the fear of making mistakes.
* **Examples / Variations:**
  * Teacher creates a set of capital and lowercase letter manipulatives to use in class (e.g. small or large paper, cardboard, bottlecaps, stones)
  * Teacher uses common ending word families to display patterns (e.g. "flip, sip, hip, lip" "hat, mat, bat, cat" "lot, rot, hot, dot" "run, sun, bun, fun")
  * Teacher uses blending squares to help students "tap the sounds"
  * Teacher ties a string from one end of the classroom to the other and place letter cards that fold on the line to create a blending line
  * Students work in groups to create a word then share and blend it with the class
* **Reflection Prompt:** How can using manipulatives to build words change student confidence?
* **Teacher Voice:** "My students treated reading like a puzzle instead of a test." or "Seeing the word change made decoding click."
