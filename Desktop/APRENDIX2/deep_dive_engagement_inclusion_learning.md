### 2. Learning Objectives (2–3)

* Teachers use inclusive strategies to keep all students engaged in the learning process, preventing disruptive behavior.  

* Teachers offer multiple ways for children to participate and respond during classroom activities. 

* Teachers communicate clear learning goals that help children feel focused, calm, and ready to learn.


### 3. Teacher Motivations & Pain Points

* “I want my class to feel active and welcoming, not noisy and chaotic."

* “I have students of many ages and levels, and I struggle to keep everyone engaged.”

* “My students become restless and distracted during long lessons.”

* “Some children understand quickly, while others get lost and then act out.”

### 4. Module Rules

* Emphasize accessibility for mixed-age, mixed-language classrooms  
* Focus on prevention, not responding to misbehavior  
* If teachers use manipulatives or teaching aids from the environment, they must be safe and appropriate  
* When using movement-based learning strategies, students should have space to move their body without physically hurting themselves or another student

### 5. Key Concepts

* **HC_M4_CON1 / Engagement prevents disruption.** When students are actively engaged, they are less likely to become bored, frustrated, or disruptive. Engagement keeps children focused on learning and gives them a positive way to use their energy. Shared ownership in the classroom fosters a sense of engagement not only in academics, but also positive behaviors. In crowded or stressful classrooms, active engagement helps prevent behavior challenges before they begin. Lessons that invite participation help students to feel a sense of involvement and belonging. 

* **HC_M4_CON2 / Differentiation supports understanding for all learners.** Children learn in different ways and at different speeds. Inclusive teaching practices ensure that classroom lessons and activities are accessible and appropriate for all learners, even when language or ability differs. Using differentiation during learning increases participation and student engagement. In low-resource or crisis-affected classrooms, inclusive practices reduce confusion and anxiety, allowing learners above, below, or on grade-level to interact with developmentally appropriate academic content. 

* **HC_M4_CON3 / Active learning builds focus and curiosity.** Movement, discussion, and hands-on activities help children manage their energy and attention. Active learning supports emotional regulation by giving students safe outlets for movement and expression. This is especially important in classrooms where students have experienced stress or disruption. When learning is active and inclusive, students stay focused longer and become invested in their schooling.

* **HC\_M4\_CON4 / Clear learning goals create readiness and confidence**. Children who understand what they are learning and why it matters feel more prepared and confident in the classroom. Clear goals reduce uncertainty and help students focus their attention throughout an activity or lesson. When students know the goal, they can better track their own progress and effort, which helps them stay engaged and feel successful in their learning. Also, clear learning goals help teachers stay focused and organized during large topics or units. 

### 6. Strategies

#### HC_M4_STRAT1 / Strategy 1: Multiple Ways to Respond

* **Description:** Offer different ways for children to show their understanding.

* **Expanded Explanation:** When children can choose how to respond, they feel more capable and less frustrated during instruction. Choices allow children to feel respected and included. Teachers can prompt students to answer questions through speaking, writing, drawing, acting, etc. Offering multiple ways to respond may not be applicable for all lessons or activities. But if teachers try to offer choice at least once per day, they can help students build confidence and agency. Teachers should make sure that all students are academically challenged in a way that promotes each child’s learning styles (e.g. Students draw their morning routine, act out their morning routine, or write a list detailing the routine.)Students should be encouraged to choose responses that support their interests and abilities, yet still grow their understanding of the lesson topic. 

* **Examples / Variations:** 

  * Students act out a story or write a paragraph to summarize a book

  * Students draw a model or write an equation to model a math problem

  * Students make flashcards, write a story, or role play to practice vocabulary words

  * Students work individually, in pairs, or in a small group to review for a test

  * Students collaborate with others who chose the same response style in pairs or small groups 

  * Teacher allows students who utilized different response styles to share with the whole class

* **Reflection Prompt:** Which students in your class might participate more if they can choose how to respond?

* **Quiz Item:** 

  * How does giving students choices in how they respond help behavior? (Keywords: reduces frustration, participation, engagement)

* **Teacher Voice:**  “More students stay calm and involved when they have more ways to respond.”

#### HC_M4_STRAT2 / Strategy 2: Simple Teaching Aids

* **Description:** Use simple teaching aids to support learning and understanding.

* **Expanded Explanation:** Gestures, pictures, local objects, demonstrations, etc. are all simple teaching aids that reduce language barriers and support children making meaningful connections to content. Teaching aids can be cost-efficient, or even free. Displaying posters on the classroom wall helps students recall information and helps create a beautiful learning environment. Additionally, teachers can use the environment to collect teaching aids for the classroom, like sticks, leaves, and stones. If teachers use objects from the environment, they must ensure that they are safe and appropriate for students. Simple teaching aids serve as manipulatives that make learning hands-on and can be utilized in all subjects. Teachers can use community relationships to obtain teaching aids like bottlecaps, plastic bottles, cardboard, fabric scraps, etc. 

* **Examples / Variations:** 

  * Students help design or draw a poster for key ideas for a lesson

  * Work with other teachers to create or collect simple teaching aids 

  * Establish a teaching material spot in the classroom where manipulatives are stored

  * Use stones to help solve math problems and to segment sounds in words

  * Create and display posters of different shapes and their names 

  * Students draw step-by-step pictures of how to complete tasks (e.g. how to wash your hands, how to enter the classroom, how to line-up at the door)

  * Use sticks to model place value (e.g. 10 single sticks make a bundle, 10 bundles make a 100)

* **Reflection Prompt:** What teaching aids could I add to one lesson for tomorrow?

* **Quiz Item:** 

  * What are simple teaching aids you can find in your community? (Keywords: cardboard, fabric, bottles, bottle caps, stones)

  * How can simple teaching aids help keep students engaged? (Keywords: meaningful connections, reduce frustration, focused, better understanding, fun)

* **Teacher Voice:** “Using visuals and objects helped students understand faster.”

#### HC_M4_STRAT3 / Strategy 3: Different Types of Questions

* **Description:** Ask a variety of assessing and advancing questions to keep all students thinking.

* **Expanded Explanation:** Different types of questions serve different purposes for teachers and students. Closed questions are simple and let teachers check student understanding of a topic (e.g. “What does 5 − 2 equal?,” “What is the next shape in the pattern?,” “Does the sentence end with a period?”). Closed questions have one answer only and they may be yes/no questions. By contrast, open questions are deeper questions that encourage critical thinking. Open questions can have many different answers. “What is a word that rhymes with mat?,” “Our story ended with the boy arriving at the football field. What do you think happened next?,” “How would you draw a house with only squares and triangles?”). Using a mixture of closed and open questions during lessons keeps all learners engaged. Teachers can ask students who are becoming distracted or bored a question to reengage them in a lesson. Teachers can also check student understanding of content when they ask different types of questions and should observe which students may need additional support for specific learning goals. 

* **Examples / Variations:** 

  * Start whole-class questioning/discussions by asking simple questions then begin asking deeper, advancing questions to promote critical thinking skills.

  * Use pair or small group discussion for deep questions then allow some pairs/groups to share with the whole class.

  * Prewrite questions before class to ensure different types are used.

  * Ask students a deep question and walk away, giving them time to think and collaborate with peers.

  * Verbally model sentence frames to help students share their thoughts (e.g. “I think \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ because\_\_\_\_\_.” or “This reminds me of \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_.”) . Teachers can also write sentence frames on the board. 

* **Reflection Prompt:** What is a simple and deep question I can ask during my next lesson?

* **Quiz Item:** How do different questions keep students engaged? (Keywords: inclusion, confidence, critical thinking, participation)

* **Teacher Voice:** “When I asked different types of questions, more students raised their hands.”

#### HC_M4_STRAT4 / Strategy 4: Movement-Based Learning

* **Description:** Safe movement manages energy. 

* **Expanded Explanation:** Movement helps children release energy and refocus their attention. In crowded classrooms, students can become highly stimulated, making it difficult to sit still. Simple, safe movements keep students engaged without causing disruption and chaos. Teachers can incorporate movements—like standing, pointing, or acting—into lessons to engage students and make learning fun. When using movement in the classroom, teachers need to model how to move safely to protect oneself and others. Teachers can add movements to lessons that are longer to reduce student restlessness.   

* **Examples / Variations:** 

  * Teachers display word cards around the classroom (e.g. agree/disagree, true/false, A/B/C/D), ask questions, and have students point/walk to their answers

  * Lead students to stand-up and stretch between the whole-class instruction and independent work

  * Teacher leads class in a song before starting a lesson

* **Reflection Prompt:** Is there a specific time during the school day when your students need movement most?

* **Quiz Item:** 

  * When is movement-based learning most helpful? (Options: When students are restless/ only during games/ during independent work)

  * Movement-based learning means losing control of the class. (Options: True/ false)

* **Teacher Voice:** “Using small movements during my lessons gave students a safe way to release energy and stay focused.”

#### HC_M4_STRAT5 / Strategy 5: Clear Learning Goals

* **Description:** State what children are learning and why it matters. 

* **Expanded Explanation:** When students know the purpose of a lesson, they are less anxious and more engaged. Simple, child-friendly goals support engagement and motivation. Teachers should write the learning goal on the board/slate/paper at the beginning of each lesson. Then they can read it and discuss with the class why it matters for their academic learning. Teachers should provide a real-world connection when explaining learning goals when applicable. Learning goals should begin with, “I can…,” followed by an action word (e.g. name, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate, create) and what the students should be able to do by the end of the lesson. An example learning goal is “I can count from 0 to 30.”  Learning goals should be simple to read and measurable. Teachers should be able to assess if students met a learning goal by the end of a lesson. Students can use the learning goal to understand the purpose of a lesson and self evaluate. Clear goals reduce confusion and promote learner readiness. 

* **Examples / Variations:** 

  * Display the learning goal in written or picture form

  * Ask students to use gestures at the end of a lesson to self-assess their understanding (thumbs up, middle, down)

  * Allow students to discuss in pairs/groups how the lesson goal could be applied outside of school

  * Write different action words on cards and hang them around the classroom 

* **Reflection Prompt:** How would my class’s behavior improve with clear learning goals?

* **Quiz Item:** 

  * Pick the best option. I should review a lesson goal at the… (Options: beginning of a lesson only/ end of a lesson only/ beginning and end of a lesson)

  * How do students feel when they know the learning goal? (Keywords: confident, prepared, ready to learn, focused)

* **Teacher Voice:** “Sharing clear goals helped my students feel prepared and ready to learn.”
