### 2. Learning Objectives (2–3)

* Teachers explain reading as a process that combines sounds, words, meanings, and relationships.
* Teachers use short, predictable routines that support confidence and participation in reading lessons.
* Teachers practice simple oral and written strategies that build understanding, not memorization.

### 3. Module Rules

* Emphasize understanding over memorization.
* Avoid examples that reward speed or perfection.
* Avoid using "manipulating" when talking about sounds.

### 4. Key Concepts

* **READING_M1_CON1 / Reading is more than decoding**. Reading is based on understanding ideas, pictures, feelings, and messages within print. Although learning how to say words correctly or quickly is an important fluency skill, it is not the final goal. When reading only focuses on accuracy, children may feel anxious or afraid to make mistakes. However, when students experience the joy of reading, they may see it as a way to understand the world around them in a new and important way. Teaching reading as meaning helps learners feel capable and motivated to keep trying even when it may be difficult.

* **READING_M1_CON2 / Oral language builds reading**. Before children can read words on a page, they need opportunities to talk, listen, and explain both their ideas and the ideas of others. Oral language supports overall reading development. When students describe pictures, tell stories, or respond to questions in class, they are practicing the same skills they will use to understand text. This is especially important in low-resource classrooms where books may be limited. Oral language activities are inclusive ways to allow every child to participate in reading class, even if they are not yet reading independently. Building oral language is an accessible strategy to assist learners as they begin to interact with written text.

* **READING_M1_CON3 / Sounds connect speech to print**. All words are made of sounds. Students need time and practice to become comfortable playing with those sounds as they learn how to read. Blending and segmenting sounds help students learn how written words work and effectively read new words, rather than memorizing them. These skills are especially important in classrooms where children have different levels of reading and limited access to books. When students have strategies to help them read it builds independence, persistence, and confidence.

* **READING_M1_CON4 / Predictable routines promote learning.** When reading instruction and activities follow predictable patterns, children feel safer and more willing to participate. Knowing what to expect reduces anxiety and allows students to focus on learning instead of worrying about making mistakes. A calm, consistent structure makes reading feel possible rather than overwhelming. As students interact consistently with reading, they gain confidence and develop skills. Learning to read can be challenging, but regular practice with sounds, words, print, and oral language helps reading become easier over time.

#### READING_M1_STRAT1 / Strategy 1: Daily Picture Talk

📷 Send this image first, then deliver strategy content:
![](https://i.imgur.com/T6M7ZD7.jpeg)

* **Description:** Daily routine where learners are shown a picture/drawing and asked to describe what they see.
* **Expanded Explanation:** The first form of reading is looking at pictures; children can connect to an image and draw meaning from it. Daily picture walks should be done at the beginning of reading lessons to establish a predictable routine. The habitual practice of using oral language to communicate thoughts at the beginning of reading lessons helps students build vocabulary and comprehension skills. Giving students the opportunity to express their ideas also builds confidence. This strategy can be implemented in low resource classrooms, as a simple drawing on the board is enough to spark discussion. Teachers can utilize daily picture talks as a way to build student background knowledge (e.g. "Remember when we looked at the picture of a garden? Today we will learn why plants, like vegetables in a garden, need sunlight"). During picture talks, teachers are not asking simple questions, but guiding and prompting student discussion with deep questions (e.g. "I heard Mo say that the picture showed a boy getting off the bus. Where do you think he may be going?").
* **Examples / Variations:**
  * Teacher brings in an object, or displays a picture on their mobile phone instead of drawing a picture
  * Teacher shows a picture of a market and asks students to think about their five senses (e.g. "What do you hear/smell/taste/see/feel?")
  * Teacher uses pair and share or small groups for students to share ideas with a few peers before sharing with the entire class
  * Teacher asks students to draw a picture they would like to use as the daily picture talk
  * Teacher uses locally relevant pictures/objects (e.g. Shows a picture of an umbrella after a rain storm and asks questions like "When would you use this and why?" or "Who can tell me a time when they did not have an umbrella, but needed one?")
* **Reflection Prompt:** What is a picture talk you could do at the start of your next reading lesson?
* **Teacher Voice:** "When we start with a picture, students are more engaged in the learning process."

#### READING_M1_STRAT2 / Strategy 2: Sound Awareness Warm-Ups

📷 Send this image first, then deliver strategy content:
![](https://i.imgur.com/ealjwLV.jpeg)

* **Description:** Quick oral games that help students interact with letters and sounds.
* **Expanded Explanation:** Sound is the basis of spoken and written language, so playing with sounds is a key part of learning to read. Sound awareness warm-ups help students prepare their brains for reading and build foundational understandings of how sounds work in their language and how those sounds connect to letters. Sound awareness warm-ups should be interactive; incorporating movement is a great way to keep students engaged (e.g. The teacher says a word and students jump once if the word ends with a /t/ sound). Equally important, letter-sound awareness warm-ups connect a sound directly to the letter (e.g. The teacher says a word and students jump if the word begins with the letter "m"). These playful activities allow students to make mistakes in a low stakes setting. Teachers must be actively listening to students during these warm-ups. When teaching phonics, it is important that students are pronouncing sounds correctly. When leading the whole class in these warm-ups, the teacher will not be able to hear each individual student but they will be able to acknowledge the general sound the class says, making sure it is correct.
* **Examples / Variations:**
  * **Sound Awareness:** Teacher makes sounds while students have their eyes closed (e.g. knocks on wall, claps, jumps) and students guess the sound
  * **Sound Awareness:** Teacher says a word then students repeat the word and clap the syllables (e.g. Teacher says "baby," students say "baby," students clap and say *"ba" "by"*, 2 syllables)
  * **Sound Awareness:** Students clap if the teacher says two words that rhyme (e.g. Teacher says "bat, rat" and students clap once). If the words do not rhyme, the students provide a rhyming word (e.g. Teacher says "light, lamp" and students say "right")
  * **Sound Awareness:** Students stand up if a word has a medial sound of /o/ and sit down if the word does not have the medial /o/ sound (e.g. Teacher says "rot" and students stand up, teacher says "bed" and students sit down)
  * **Sound Awareness:** Teacher says a word and students say the first (or last) sound they hear (e.g. Teacher says "sat" and students say "/s/")
  * **Letter-Sound Awareness:** Teacher says a letter sound and students guess what letter it is (e.g. Teacher says "/m/" and students guess the letter "m")
  * **Letter-Sound Awareness:** Teacher says a word and students say the letter that the word ends with (e.g. Teacher says "rain" and students say "n")
* **Delivery note:** This strategy covers two related but distinct skills — draw at least one example from **Sound Awareness** and one from **Letter-Sound Awareness** above, rather than only one type, since the Expanded Explanation treats both as "equally important."
* **Reflection Prompt:** Where in the school day could you add a sound awareness warm-up?
* **Teacher Voice:** "I like to see my students have fun when practicing sounds."

#### READING_M1_STRAT3 / Strategy 3: Blending and Segmenting Sounds

📷 Send this image first, then deliver strategy content:
![](https://i.imgur.com/jLBFmgY.jpeg)

* **Description:** Practicing how to put sounds together to make words and how to break words into smaller sounds
* **Expanded Explanation:** Blending and segmenting sounds is where students build on their knowledge of letters and letter sounds to begin reading printed words. Teachers are helping students use their letter-sound knowledge to decode words, increasing vocabulary and also forming the foundation for writing. Blending shows learners that putting sounds together creates words. Teachers provide students with only letters or sounds and students blend the sounds together to make a word (e.g. /h//a//t/, hat). Segmenting shows learners that words can be broken down into sounds. Teachers provide students with a word and students segment each sound they hear (e.g. hat, /h//a//t/). Both strategies are important for students to learn how to respond when coming across words that are new or unfamiliar. This is building resilience and problem solving skills. An important resource for blending and segmenting words is letter cards. Teachers can make these on posters or cardboard for durability.
* **Examples / Variations:**
  * Chopping sounds- (e.g. Teacher says a simple word like "fed", each student puts their hands together and moves them up and down to make a chop. Students chop and say each sound they hear /f//e//d/, then students say the word again moving their hands from left to right, blending the sounds.)
  * Arm blending- students use their shoulder, elbows, and wrists to blend simple words (e.g. Teachers says /w//e//t/, students tap their shoulder and say /w/, tap their elbow and say /e/, tap their wrist and say /t/, slide hand from shoulder to wrist saying "wet")
  * Use letters cards to create real and nonsense words (e.g. Teacher displays the word "pof" using letter cards. Students segment the sounds and say the word. Teacher asks "Is this a real or nonsense word?" Students say "nonsense" and provide a way to make it real "change the /f/ to /t/, /p//o//t/ pot").
  * Use letter cards to blend and segment words in small groups (e.g. Teacher displays the word "flip." One at a time, students touch each letter card and say the corresponding sound then blend the sounds to say the word "/f//l//i//p/ flip").
* **Reflection Prompt:** What is a resource you could create to help students practice blending and segmenting?
* **Teacher Voice:** "My students feel proud when they read a word by themselves."

#### READING_M1_STRAT4 / Strategy 4: Link Words to Meaning

* **Description:** Demonstrating word meaning by making connections and prompting discussion
* **Expanded Explanation:** Linking words to their meaning allows students to understand, or comprehend, what they are reading. This encourages students to critically think about what they are reading instead of memorizing. When teachers regularly ask what a word means or invites students to show meaning with their faces, hands, or actions, students make meaningful connections to the word (e.g. "Whisper is a quiet way of talking. Everyone whisper your name. Now, can you whisper something nice to the person next to you?"). Introducing new vocabulary to students is important and can be done through reading or telling stories. Teachers prompt students to raise their hands when they hear a new word, so the class can review it together. When learning new vocabulary is a regular part of reading class, it reduces students' fear of being wrong and helps them see themselves as thoughtful readers.
* **Examples / Variations:**
  * Teacher does a quick comprehension check during the lesson to review vocabulary and check student understanding (e.g. "Have you heard any words that are new? What do you think it means?")
  * Write the new words on the board and review the meaning throughout the school day or week
  * Create a "word wall" by writing new terms on paper and posting them to a designated wall in the classroom. When there is extra time in class, the teacher can lead the students in reviewing the words and their meanings.
  * Ask students to draw a picture that represents a new word (e.g. student draws a picture of a kid putting notebooks into a backpack to show the word "packing")
  * Ask students to act out the meaning of a new word (e.g. students act like they are preparing tea to show the word "boil")
* **Reflection Prompt:** When do students seem confused even after reading a word correctly?
* **Teacher Voice:** "I realized some students were reading without understanding."

#### READING_M1_STRAT5 / Strategy 5: Mini Shared Writing

📷 Send this image first, then deliver strategy content:
![](https://i.imgur.com/E9XcFmg.jpeg)

* **Description:** Writing a sentence with students using their spoken ideas
* **Expanded Explanation:** Shared writing helps students visualize their spoken words and ideas become print. When teachers share a short oral story or read-aloud, they can engage their students in a discussion to think about what they read as a class (e.g. "What happened in our story?" "What was the lesson?" "What happened at the beginning/middle/end?"). Next, teachers can write a sentence using the learner's ideas on the board, narrating their writing and engaging learners to help them spell by stretching out words. Once the sentence is finished, the class can read the sentence together. This models to students how writing is connected to reading. Since the sentence was written collaboratively, students feel included and proud, which strengthens confidence and motivation to read.
* **Examples / Variations:**
  * Teacher narrates thinking to the class ( e.g."If I want to start a sentence, my first word must begin with?. . . Yes, you are right. It must begin with a capital letter.", "The next word I need to write is 'land.' Stretch this word out with me, /l//a//n//d/. The first sound I hear is /l/, I should write 'l'. . .)
  * Teacher allows students to share their ideas about the story in pairs or small groups before sharing with the whole class
  * Students write the sentence in notebooks/slates/air as the teacher is writing on the board
  * Teacher provides a specific idea for the class sentence (e.g. "I want our sentence to summarize the story" or "I want our sentence to tell someone the lesson of the story" or "Our sentence should tell someone the problem in the story.")
  * Teacher purposefully makes a mistake and asks students to check their work (e.g. Teacher writes a misspells a word, "We stretched out 'dinner' but I do not know if it is correct." "Thank you for helping me, sometimes we make mistakes when writing. When we feel unsure, it is ok to ask for help").
  * Teacher reviews how to write a nice sentence before beginning to write on the board (e.g. "When I write a sentence what should I do?. . . Yes, I need to use punctuation at the end, capital letter at the beginning, spaces in between my words, and nice handwriting.")
* **Reflection Prompt:** How might students react when their ideas become written words?
* **Teacher Voice:** "My students are excited to read sentences we make together as a class."
