## LEARNING OBJECTIVES

- Establish consistent classroom routines that promote physical and emotional safety for all children
- Apply positive, restorative approaches to behavior guidance that preserve children's dignity
- Create an inclusive, participatory classroom environment where every child feels seen and protected

## TEACHER MOTIVATIONS & PAIN POINTS

- "My class is loud and crowded. I spend so much energy managing behavior that I don't have time to think about whether children feel safe."
- "I was taught to use punishment to keep order. I'm not always sure what else works, especially with large groups."
- "Some of my students have seen terrible things. I don't know how to make school feel safe for them when I can barely manage the basics."
- "There are children in my class who are always on the periphery — quiet, invisible. I worry about them but I don't know how to reach them."

- Inclusion must be treated as a protective factor, not an add-on. Do not frame inclusive practices as additional effort for teachers — frame them as integral to a safe classroom.
- Do not suggest strategies that require additional materials, printed resources, or specialist support to implement.

## INTERNAL: Concepts (Agent Guidance Only)

> **Do NOT show these concepts to the user.** Concepts are overarching and apply across strategies. Use them to guide how you frame and deliver the strategies — tone, emphasis, why it matters — but never present concepts as separate content. The user sees only strategies.

### CON1 — The Classroom Environment Is a Safeguarding Tool

A classroom that feels safe, predictable, and welcoming reduces vulnerability. When children know what to expect, trust their teacher, and feel they belong, they are more likely to disclose concerns and less likely to be isolated or targeted. In crisis-affected contexts where home and community environments may be unpredictable, the classroom can be a rare source of stability.

### CON2 — Punishment Undermines Protection

Corporal punishment, public shaming, and exclusion do not make classrooms safer — they erode the trust children need to seek help. Children who fear their teacher are less likely to disclose harm, report peer conflict, or show signs of distress openly. Positive, dignity-preserving discipline builds the relational safety that makes safeguarding possible.

### CON3 — Exclusion Is a Risk Factor

Children who feel invisible, unwelcome, or disconnected from their peers are more vulnerable to harm and less likely to receive help when they need it. Marginalized children, including those with disabilities, from minority groups, or experiencing displacement, often fall outside the informal networks of peer support that protect other children. Inclusion is not a separate agenda from safeguarding — it is part of it.

### CON4 — Children Who Understand Their Rights Are Safer

Children who know they have the right to be safe, to say no, and to confide in a trusted adult are better equipped to recognize and report harm. Age-appropriate conversations about body autonomy and personal boundaries give children language and permission they may not receive anywhere else, particularly in contexts where discussing these topics is culturally unfamiliar or taboo.

### SAFEGUARDING_M2_STRAT1 — Establish Protective Routines

**Description:** Introduce consistent, predictable classroom structures that create natural opportunities to notice when a child's behavior or wellbeing has changed.

**Expanded explanation:** Protective routines are not primarily about classroom management — they are about creating the conditions under which harm becomes visible and trust becomes possible. When the same structures repeat each day, deviations stand out: a child who suddenly disengages from a familiar role, withdraws from peers they are normally close to, or resists a transition they usually handle well is sending a signal. In crisis-affected contexts where children's lives outside school may be chaotic and unpredictable, consistent classroom structures also reduce the background anxiety that can mask or amplify signs of distress, making it easier to distinguish a child who is struggling from one who is simply having a hard day.

**Examples / Variations:**
- Assign each child a consistent daily role (greeter, material collector, closer — rotating each month). When a child who normally engages with their role suddenly refuses or forgets, treat this as a signal worth a quiet follow-up, not a disciplinary moment.
- Use a consistent opening sequence each day. If a child who always participates in the opening ritual is suddenly absent from it — physically present but disengaged — note it and observe further.
- Use a buddy system not only to build cooperation, but also to ensure no child is ever consistently alone or unwitnessed. A child whose buddy reports they seem sad or scared is a point to note and observe, not just a social concern.
- In multi-age classrooms, pair older and younger children in ways that give the teacher visibility across the full room. Notice if an older child suddenly becomes avoidant or aggressive toward a younger one they previously connected well with.

**Reflection prompt:** Which routine in your classroom best helps you notice when something has changed for a child?

**Teacher Voice:** "I thought routines were about keeping order. Now I see them as a way of staying close enough to my students that I notice when something is wrong."

### SAFEGUARDING_M2_STRAT2 — Replace Punishment with Positive Guidance

**Description:** Respond to challenging behavior with restorative, child-centered approaches that preserve dignity and build trust rather than fear.

**Expanded explanation:** When teachers use corporal punishment, shaming, or exclusion, they send children the message that adults respond to difficulty with harm, which is precisely the dynamic safeguarding seeks to interrupt. Positive guidance means addressing behavior in ways that help children understand expectations, repair relationships, and maintain their sense of worth. This approach is not about permissiveness; it is about maintaining trust and safety that make children willing to seek help when they need it. In crowded, high-stress classrooms, simple restorative responses — calm redirection, private conversations, acknowledgement of effort — can shift the emotional tone of the entire room. Maintaining appropriate boundaries between educators and students is also part of this. Teachers should be mindful of situations that could blur those boundaries: conducting extended one-on-one interactions with a child in a private or unobserved space, developing relationships with individual students that are markedly more personal than with others, or using a position of authority to access a child's personal information beyond what is necessary. These are not always signs of harm, but they are risk factors that good safeguarding practice keeps visible. A simple principle: interactions that would concern a parent or supervisor if they saw them should be reconsidered.

**Examples / Variations:**
- Instead of shouting at a disruptive child, move close, lower your voice, and say quietly: "I've noticed you're having a hard time. Let's talk after class." This avoids public shame while still addressing the behavior.
- When two children conflict, bring them together privately and ask each to share what happened and what they need, then agree on one step forward together.
- Acknowledge positive behavior explicitly and specifically: "I saw how you helped your classmate find their place — that was kind!" This reinforces the behavior you want to see without comparison or competition.
- For children who are frequently disruptive, look for an underlying cause before responding to the behavior. Disruption is often a sign of need or distress, not defiance.

**Reflection prompt:** Think of a time you responded to a child's behavior in a way you felt good about. What did you do, and what made it work?

**Teacher Voice:** "In my training, I was told that firm discipline keeps children in line. But I've seen that the children who get punished most are often the ones who need the most support."

### SAFEGUARDING_M2_STRAT3 — Include Every Child

**Description:** Make consistent efforts to ensure that marginalized children — including those with disabilities or experiencing displacement — are included as part of classroom life.

**Expanded explanation:** Children who feel invisible or excluded are more vulnerable to harm and less likely to seek help when something is wrong. Inclusion is not about treating every child identically — it is about ensuring every child has genuine access to participation, connection, and care. In crisis-affected classrooms, some children arrive with additional barriers: language differences, interrupted schooling, disability, or the social stigma of displacement. Small, deliberate acts of inclusion (ensuring a child can see the board, pairing a newcomer with a welcoming peer, using gestures alongside spoken instructions) strengthen the protective environment for everyone.

**Examples / Variations:**
- When introducing a new activity, check that children with visual or hearing difficulties are seated where they can fully participate — not placed at the back by default.
- When a new or displaced child joins the class, assign a peer welcome buddy for their first week to ease integration and reduce isolation.
- Use multiple ways of giving instructions — spoken, gestured, and demonstrated — so children with language differences or learning difficulties are not left behind.
- In multi-age classrooms, create mixed-age tasks where different contributions are equally valued, so older or more advanced children are not always positioned above others.

**Reflection prompt:** What is one thing you could do this week to bring a quieter or more isolated child more fully into classroom life?

**Teacher Voice:** "There's a girl in my class who never speaks. I assumed she was just shy, but now I wonder if she feels like she doesn't belong here."

### SAFEGUARDING_M2_STRAT4 — Teach Body Autonomy Basics

**Description:** Introduce age-appropriate concepts around personal boundaries and the right to be free from any harmful physical contact — including but not limited to sexual harm — and how to seek help from a trusted adult.

**Expanded explanation:** Body autonomy means every child has the right to feel physically safe, and that no form of harmful touch — whether violent, sexual, or otherwise — is acceptable or the child's fault. Many children in crisis-affected contexts have never been explicitly told this, and may have normalized physical harm as something adults are permitted to do. The safe/unsafe touch framework gives children simple, clear language to recognize when something is wrong across a wide range of situations — a beating, an unwanted sexual touch, or any contact that leaves them feeling scared or hurt. These messages are most effective when woven into normal classroom life rather than delivered as a one-off lesson, and when teachers frame them as empowering rather than alarming.

**Examples / Variations:**
- Teach the concept of "safe touch" and "unsafe touch" using simple, clear language appropriate to the children's age: "A safe touch makes you feel comfortable and respected. An unsafe touch makes you feel scared or confused."
- Role play scenarios where children practice saying "no" and "stop" clearly and confidently — normalize this as a skill, not rudeness.
- Identify with children who the trusted adults are in their lives (teacher, parent, community leader) and remind them regularly: "If something makes you feel unsafe, you can always tell me or another grownup you trust."
- For younger children, use a simple body outline drawn on the board to explain which parts of the body are private and always belong to them alone.

**Reflection prompt:** What has held you back from talking with your students about personal safety or trusted adults?

**Teacher Voice:** "It felt uncomfortable to me at first — we don't usually talk about these things openly. But I realized that silence is what puts children at risk."

### DIY_ACTIVITY_1: Buddy Check

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **Materials** | None |
| **Steps** | Assign each child a buddy and prioritize pairing children who may be isolated with a calm, welcoming peer. Each morning, give buddies 60 seconds to greet each other and share how they are feeling that day. As buddies talk, circulate quietly and observe. Notice any pairs where one child is disengaged or visibly upset. Follow up privately after class. Rotate buddy pairs weekly so that no child's wellbeing depends on a single peer. |
| **Variation** | For very large classes, create buddy groups of three. For younger children, simplify the question to "Are you okay today? Thumbs up or thumbs down." |
| **Observation** | A child who consistently signals distress to their buddy but not the teacher may not feel safe enough yet to disclose directly. The buddy system creates an indirect channel that the teacher can follow up on gently. |
| **Time** | 5 min |

### DIY_ACTIVITY_2: Everyone Has a Voice

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **Materials** | Small stones, sticks, or bottle caps (one per child) |
| **Steps** | Give each child one stone (or stick, cap, etc.) at the beginning of an activity. Explain: when you contribute (answer a question, share an idea, help a peer), you place your stone in the center. The goal is for every stone to be in the center by the end of the activity. Observe who places their stone easily, who holds back, and who looks to others for permission or validation. |
| **Variation** | For younger children, simplify to a thumbs up when they have contributed something. For larger classes, do this in small groups rather than as a whole class. |
| **Observation** | This activity surfaces children who consistently hold back — this may be out of shyness, or because they do not feel they belong or that their contribution isn't welcome. |
| **Time** | 10–15 min, embedded in any lesson activity |
