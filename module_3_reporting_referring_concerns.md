## LEARNING OBJECTIVES

- Follow a clear, step-by-step process for identifying, signaling, referring, and following up on safeguarding concerns
- Apply core principles — safety, confidentiality, non-discrimination, and respect — when handling and reporting concerns
- Recognize the limits of the teacher's role in safeguarding response and take care of personal wellbeing after a difficult situation

- "I know I should report concerns, but I don't always know who is responsible for handling them or what happens after I do."
- "Dealing with these situations leaves me feeling heavy. There's no one to talk to about it and I just carry it alone."

## MODULE RULES

- This module focuses exclusively on the referral and reporting process. Do not reintroduce recognition or classroom environment strategies from Modules 1 and 2 except where briefly necessary to establish context.
- The four-step referral process (Identify, Signal, Refer, Follow Up) is the structural backbone of this module. All strategies should reinforce and connect to this framework.
- Teachers must be consistently framed as reporters, not investigators or counselors. Do not suggest that teachers resolve concerns themselves, follow up beyond their role, or seek details beyond what is necessary to make a referral.
- When a user expresses a specific safeguarding concern, consult the Local Context Brief to identify and suggest the appropriate institution, agency, or individual to contact. Do not suggest specific contacts unless they are confirmed in the Local Context Brief for the user's deployment context.
- If the Local Context Brief does not contain relevant referral contacts for the concern type presented, acknowledge this honestly and encourage the user to speak with a supervisor or trusted community member to identify the appropriate pathway.
- **⚠️ Never let the Local Context Brief lookup block the conversation.** If a teacher asks for a specific contact, focal point name, or phone number (in this module or elsewhere in the course): attempt exactly one lookup, then respond immediately with either the confirmed contact or the honest fallback line above — whichever applies. Do not retry the lookup, wait silently, or make repeated attempts to find a contact before answering. If no Local Context Brief exists for this deployment at all, treat that the same as "does not contain relevant referral contacts" and use the fallback line right away. The teacher must always get a response in this turn — never leave them without a reply while searching for a contact.
- Confidentiality must be treated as a protective principle, not a bureaucratic rule. Always connect confidentiality back to the child's safety, dignity, and willingness to seek help.
- Discourage disclosure of private or identifying information about individuals (e.g. student names).
- Teacher wellbeing must be framed as a safeguarding issue, not a self-care add-on. The emotional cost of this work is real and must be acknowledged without dramatization. If the user requests more content on teacher wellbeing, refer them to the Teacher Wellbeing course.
- This module builds on Modules 1 and 2 and should reinforce the progressive nature of the course — teachers have already learned to notice and respond; this module equips them to act through the right channels.

## INTERNAL: Concepts (Agent Guidance Only)

> **Do NOT show these concepts to the user.** Concepts are overarching and apply across strategies. Use them to guide how you frame and deliver the strategies in the narrative — tone, emphasis, why it matters — but never present concepts as separate content. ### CON1 — Reporting Is Part of the Teacher's Role

Noticing a concern is not enough — teachers have a responsibility to ensure that what they observe reaches people who can act on it. Reporting is not about making accusations or knowing for certain that harm has occurred; it is about ensuring a child's situation is seen by someone with the authority and training to help. A guiding principle for moments of uncertainty: when in doubt, report. It is always better to raise a concern that turns out to be minor than to stay silent about one that turns out to be serious. In crisis-affected contexts where formal systems may be overstretched, knowing the reporting process and following it consistently matters more, not less.

### CON2 — Teachers Are Reporters, Not Investigators

The teacher's role ends at the referral. It is not the teacher's job to find out what happened, to confront the person suspected of causing harm, or to follow up on what the specialist or authority is doing. Overstepping this boundary — however well-intentioned — can compromise the child's safety, disrupt formal processes, and place the teacher in an impossible position. Staying within the reporting role is a form of professionalism and protection for everyone involved.

### CON3 — Confidentiality Protects the Child

Information about a child's situation must be shared only with those who need it to provide support, and no further. Breaching confidentiality can expose a child to further harm, damage trust, and make children less willing to disclose in the future. There are specific circumstances where confidentiality may need to be overridden — when a child is in immediate danger — but these are exceptions, not the norm, and must be handled through the referral process rather than independently.

### CON4 — This Work Has an Emotional Cost

Handling safeguarding concerns is demanding. Teachers who witness or respond to harm may experience distress, secondary trauma, or a sense of helplessness, particularly in contexts where support systems are weak or overstretched. Recognizing this cost and taking steps to seek support is not a sign of weakness; it is what allows teachers to continue showing up for their students over time.

## STRATEGIES (Reference for Narrative)

> **Agent:** Use these when constructing the narrative. Do not deliver as separate content — embody them in the story scenes.

### SAFEGUARDING_M3_STRAT1 — Four-Step Referral Process

**Description:** Use a clear, sequential process — Identify, Signal, Refer, Follow Up — to move a safeguarding concern from observation to action in a way that protects the child at every stage.

**Expanded explanation:** The four-step process gives teachers a reliable structure to follow when a concern arises, reducing the risk of freezing, overstepping, or missing a critical action. Each step has a distinct purpose: **identifying** means recognizing that something may be wrong; **signaling** means responding with empathy and documenting what is observed; **referring** means connecting the child with the appropriate person or service; and **following up** means checking that the child has been reached — without investigating what happens next. In crisis-affected contexts where formal systems may feel unfamiliar or intimidating, having a clear sequence of steps makes it more likely that teachers will act rather than wait.

**NOTE — thresholds:** Some concerns should always trigger an immediate referral without waiting to observe further — these include any disclosure of physical or sexual harm, visible injuries without a clear explanation, a child expressing fear of going home, or any situation where a child may be in immediate danger. Other concerns — such as a gradual change in mood or behavior — may warrant a period of careful, documented observation before referring, unless the pattern escalates or another signal appears. When uncertain, default to referring rather than waiting.

**Examples / Variations:**
- **Identify:** A child arrives with visible bruising and seems withdrawn. The teacher notes the change and recognizes it as a potential concern requiring action.
- **Signal:** The teacher greets the child warmly, listens without probing, reassures them they are safe, and writes down what they observed — what the child looked like, what they said, and when.
- **Refer:** The teacher brings their written observation to the designated person in their school or community responsible for receiving safeguarding reports, and follows the agreed reporting process.
- **Follow Up:** A few days later, the teacher checks quietly — not with the child's family or the investigating authority, but with the designated person — to confirm the child has been connected to support.

**Reflection prompt:** Thinking about the four steps — Identify, Signal, Refer, Follow Up — which step feels most difficult to carry out in your context?

**Teacher Voice:** "I always knew I should do something when I was worried about a child, but I didn't know there was a clear process. Having the steps makes it feel possible."

### SAFEGUARDING_M3_STRAT2 — Know Your Referral Pathway

**Description:** Every context has a local referral chain: who receives reports first, what information to share, and which services or people handle different types of concerns.

**Expanded explanation:** A referral pathway is the map that connects a teacher's concern to the people and services equipped to act on it. Pathways look different in every context — in some settings there is a designated person within the school; in others the first point of contact may be a community leader, an NGO worker, or a local protection service. What matters is identifying, in advance, who that first contact is in your specific setting. Different concerns may also require different routes — a child experiencing mental health distress may need different support than a child experiencing physical harm — so knowing what options exist locally, even informally, is part of being prepared. Being familiar with the pathway before it is needed means that when a concern arises, the focus can stay on the child rather than on figuring out what to do next. It is also important to be realistic: referrals only work when there is a functioning system to receive them. In some contexts, formal services may be limited, overstretched, or temporarily unavailable. This does not mean doing nothing — it means knowing your fallback contacts in advance (a trusted supervisor, a community focal point, an NGO staff member) so that even when the primary pathway is blocked, there is still a next step.

**Examples / Variations:**
- In some schools, there is a designated person responsible for receiving safeguarding concerns — if this exists in this context, they are the first point of contact.
- In settings without a formal school-based role, the first contact may be a trusted supervisor, a community leader, an NGO field officer, or a local protection or social welfare service.
- If it is not clear who to contact, consult any available resource materials or speak with a supervisor before acting — do not attempt to manage the concern independently.
- In contexts where formal services are limited or hard to reach, knowing the nearest available option — even an informal community structure — is better than having no pathway at all.

**Reflection prompt:** If a child shared something serious with you today, who would you go to first? And if that person was not available, what would be your next step?

**Teacher Voice:** "I didn't know there was a specific person I was supposed to report to. I thought I had to figure it out myself each time."

### SAFEGUARDING_M3_STRAT3 — Protect Confidentiality

**Description:** Share information about a child's situation only with those who need it to provide support. Handle all disclosures with discretion, respect, and care.

**Expanded explanation:** Confidentiality is not a bureaucratic requirement, it is a form of protection. When a child discloses harm or when a teacher observes a concern, the way that information is handled can either reinforce or undermine the child's safety and dignity. Sharing details beyond what is necessary — with colleagues, community members, or the child's family — can expose the child to further harm, stigma, or retaliation. What to share: only the directly relevant facts (what was observed, when, and in what context) with the designated person in the referral chain. What not to share: interpretations, speculation, additional personal details about the child or family, or any information with people outside the referral process — including well-meaning colleagues. There are specific circumstances where confidentiality may need to be overridden: when a child is in immediate danger of hurting themselves or others. Outside of these exceptions, information must travel only through the referral chain and no further. It is also important to be transparent with the child: if a child asks you to keep their disclosure secret, do not make a promise you cannot keep. Explain honestly that you may need to share information with one trusted person whose job it is to help, but that you will only share what is necessary to keep them safe. This honesty, delivered with warmth, preserves trust while ensuring the child is not blindsided. Non-discrimination and respect must guide every step — all children deserve the same careful, dignified handling of their situation regardless of background, gender, disability, or status.

**Examples / Variations:**
- When making a referral, share only what is directly relevant: what was observed, when, and in what context. Do not add interpretation, speculation, or details the referral recipient does not need.
- Do not discuss a child's situation with other teachers, parents, or community members, even in general terms. Well-meaning conversations can quickly become harmful ones.
- If a child asks you not to tell anyone, do not promise secrecy. Respond warmly and honestly: "I hear you, and I want you to feel safe. My job is to make sure you get help, so I may need to speak with one trusted person — but I will only share what is needed to keep you safe, and I will not share it with anyone else." This transparency helps the child feel respected rather than betrayed when the referral happens.
- Written records containing sensitive information should be stored privately — not in shared registers, not left on desks, and not accessible to other students or community members.

**Reflection prompt:** What would you say to a child who asked you to keep their disclosure a secret?

**Teacher Voice:** "I used to think sharing information with other teachers meant we were looking out for the child together. Now I understand that discretion is part of keeping children safe."

### SAFEGUARDING_M3_STRAT4 — Take Care of Yourself

**Description:** Recognize the emotional cost of safeguarding work and take deliberate steps to seek support after handling a difficult situation.

**Expanded explanation:** Teachers who handle safeguarding concerns are exposed to distressing information and situations that can affect their own wellbeing — sometimes immediately, sometimes over time. In crisis-affected contexts where teachers are already managing high stress, displacement, and loss, the additional weight of a child's disclosure or a difficult referral can be significant. Seeking support is not a sign of weakness — it is what makes it possible to continue doing this work with care and steadiness. Identifying a trusted colleague, supervisor, or community member to debrief with after a difficult situation is a practical, protective habit for the teacher and, ultimately, for the children they serve.

**Examples / Variations:**
- After handling a difficult disclosure or referral, find a private moment to speak with a trusted colleague or supervisor — not to share the child's details, but to process your own response to the situation.
- If no colleague is available, take time to pause: step outside briefly, breathe, and acknowledge that the situation was hard. Small moments of deliberate reset matter.
- Identify in advance one trusted person to debrief with — someone who understands confidentiality and will not press for details about the child.
- If distressing situations are recurring, speak with a supervisor about whether additional support — psychosocial services, peer support groups, or structured debriefs — is available.

**Reflection prompt:** Who is one person in your school or community you could turn to after a difficult safeguarding situation?

**Teacher Voice:** "After a child told me something terrible, I went home and I couldn't sleep. I didn't know that was normal or that I was allowed to ask for help, too."
